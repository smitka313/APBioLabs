Artificial Selection

Can extreme selection change expression of a quantitative trait in a population in one generation?

Unit 7: Natural SelectionEVO-1.FEVO-1.GScience Practice 4.B≈ 7 weeks of short sessions (wet lab) · 2 periods (online)
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    Darwin built his case for natural selection partly on what breeders already knew: if you let only certain individuals reproduce, the next generation looks different. In this lab your class raises a population of about 150 Wisconsin Fast Plants (Brassica rapa), measures a trait that varies, lets only the most extreme 10% breed, and checks whether their offspring are different.

    In the real lab this takes about seven weeks of short daily visits to a light box. Here you will do the same steps in a virtual growth chamber: count leaf hairs (trichomes) under a magnifier, build a histogram, choose parents, cross-pollinate them with a bee stick, and grow and score generation 2. Then you will design your own selection experiment.

    Learning objectives
    • EVO-1.F Explain how humans can affect diversity within a population.
    • EVO-1.G Explain the relationship between changes in the environment and evolutionary changes in the population.
    • Science Practice 4.B Describe data from a table or graph (histograms, means, standard deviation, error bars).
    • Use descriptive statistics to decide whether a population's genetic makeup changed, and design a follow-up investigation about one trait.
    Safety (wet lab)

    Plants grow under electric light banks with water reservoirs. Keep water and fertilizer solution away from wires, plugs and ballasts, and refill reservoirs carefully. Wash hands after handling soil and fertilizer.

    Background

    Variation, heredity and differential reproduction

    Key ideas
    • Natural selection needs two things: variation in a trait, and heritable variation (differences that come from genes).
    • Quantitative traits such as hair number are polygenic and vary continuously, so you describe them with histograms, means and SD.
    • Heritability h² is the genetic share of the variation: h² = VG / VP.
    • Selection differential S (how extreme the parents are) predicts the response R = h² × S, so offspring regress part of the way back.
    • A control line bred from random parents shows what happens without selection.

    Natural selection is differential reproduction: some individuals leave more surviving offspring than others because of the traits they have. If those traits are inherited, the next generation has a different genetic makeup. That change in a population's gene pool over generations is evolution. In nature the environment does the "choosing." In artificial selection, people decide which individuals breed. Corn, dogs and broccoli (a close relative of your Fast Plants) were all shaped this way.

    Selection only works when two things are true. First, individuals must vary in the trait. Second, at least part of that variation must be heritable, meaning it comes from differences in genes rather than only from differences in light, water or chance.

    Quantitative traits

    Mendel's pea traits come in clear categories, such as purple or white. Many traits instead vary on a continuum: height, number of flowers, number of hairs. These quantitative traits are usually polygenic: many genes each add a small amount, and the environment adds more variation on top. The result is a spread of values that you describe with a histogram, a mean, a median and a standard deviation.

    Fast Plants have small hairs called trichomes on their leaves and stems. Some plants have none and some have more than 50 along the edge of one leaf. Trichomes may discourage insects such as cabbage white butterfly caterpillars from eating or laying eggs, but making them costs the plant energy.

    Predicting the response to selection

    Phenotypic variance (VP) has a genetic part (VG) and an environmental part (VE). Heritability, h², is the fraction that is genetic. Breeders use it to predict how far the next generation will move:

    VP = VG + VE    h² = VG / VP
    S = (mean of selected parents) − (mean of whole population)    R = h² × S

    S is the selection differential (how extreme your chosen parents are) and R is the response (how much the offspring mean shifts). If h² were 1, offspring would be as extreme as their parents. If h² were 0, offspring would look like the original population no matter which parents you chose. Real traits fall in between, so offspring usually "regress" part of the way back toward the original mean.

    One round of strong selection can shift the mean noticeably, but it rarely changes the population all the way. Your job is to measure the shift, decide whether it is bigger than chance, and explain it.

    Key terms
    Artificial selection
    Breeders choose which individuals reproduce, so the next generation shifts toward the chosen trait.
    Quantitative trait
    A trait that varies along a continuum, such as number of hairs or height.
    Trichome
    A small leaf hair; here, the number along one leaf margin is the measured trait.
    Heritability (h²)
    The fraction of phenotypic variance that is due to genetic differences.
    Selection differential (S)
    Mean of the selected parents minus mean of the whole population.
    Response (R)
    Offspring mean minus the original population mean; R = h² × S.

    Full procedure

    Directional selection pushes a trait one way. As a class, pick the hairiest (or least hairy) 10% of the whole population. In the real lab you mark those plants and snip the tops off every other plant in your bottles, then move the selected bottles to a separate light box so no stray pollen reaches them.

    Fast Plants are self-incompatible: a flower cannot be fertilized by its own plant's pollen. On days 14–16 you collect pollen on a bee stick (a dried honeybee glued to a toothpick) and brush it across every flower of every selected plant, then repeat for three days with the same stick. The unselected plants are pollinated with a different stick so the two groups never mix.

    Evaluate and extend

    Where can you go from here?

    A common question is why these plants make hairs at all, when building them costs energy. Is there an environment where hairy plants do better? That is the start of a testable hypothesis.
