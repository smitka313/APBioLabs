Comparing DNA Sequences to Understand Evolutionary Relationships with BLAST

How can bioinformatics be used as a tool to determine evolutionary relationships and to better understand genetic diseases?

Unit 7: Natural SelectionEVO-3.BEVO-3.CSYI-3.AScience Practice 2.D≈ 2–3 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    Paleontologists working in Liaoning Province, China, have found a new fossil. Rare soft tissue survived, and DNA from it was used to sequence three genes. Your job is to decide where this species belongs on a cladogram, first from its body structure and then from its DNA.

    You will:

    1. Fill in a table of physical traits and use it to build a cladogram of six animal groups.
    2. Study the fossil and make a first hypothesis about where it fits.
    3. Search each fossil gene against a sequence database with an in-page version of BLAST. You will read scores, E-values, percent identity and the alignments themselves.
    4. Use the DNA evidence to keep or change where you placed the fossil.
    5. Design your own sequence comparison and test whether DNA differences build up like a "molecular clock."

    Background

    Cladograms, shared genes, and BLAST

    Key ideas
    • A cladogram is a hypothesis about relatedness: groups that join at a more recent node share a more recent common ancestor.
    • Cladograms are built from shared derived characters, traits that appeared in an ancestor and were passed to all its descendants.
    • Most modern trees use molecular evidence: the longer two lineages have been apart, the more their DNA differs.
    • BLAST finds local alignments and reports a bit score, an E-value (chance matches expected) and percent identity.
    • A low E-value (below about 1 × 10−4) means a hit is probably a real homolog, not a coincidence.

    A cladogram (a type of phylogenetic tree) shows a hypothesis about how species are related. The tips are species or groups. Each branch point (node) stands for a common ancestor. Two groups that join at a more recent node share a more recent common ancestor than groups that join closer to the root. You can rotate the branches around any node without changing what the tree says. Only the branching pattern matters.

    Traditional cladograms are built from shared derived characters. These are traits that appeared in an ancestor and were passed to all of its descendants. On the cladogram below, each trait is marked where it first evolved. Every group above that mark has the trait. A trait shared by many groups usually evolved earlier than a trait found in only a few.

    Molecular evidence and the molecular clock

    Today, most cladograms are built mainly from molecular evidence. When a population splits into two species, each lineage gathers its own mutations. The longer two lineages have been apart, the more differences build up in their DNA. So the percentage of matching bases between two versions of a gene tells you roughly how recently the species shared an ancestor. For example, humans and chimpanzees share more than 95% of their DNA, and humans and fruit flies share about 60%.

    BLAST: scores, E-values and percent identity

    Comparing sequences by hand is slow. BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) does it in seconds. You give it a query sequence. It finds short exact "seed" matches in every database sequence, then extends each seed into a full local alignment, allowing mismatches and gaps. Each alignment gets a score:

    Raw score S = (matches × 2) − (mismatches × 3) − (5 + 2 × gap length) for each gap

    The raw score is converted to a bit score, which can be compared between searches. The E-value is the number of alignments this good that you would expect to find by chance in a database of that size:

    E = m × n × 2−S′   (m = query length, n = database size, S′ = bit score)

    The higher the score, the closer the alignment. The lower the E-value, the less likely the match is a coincidence. A hit with E < 1 × 10−4 is usually treated as a real, related sequence (a homolog). Percent identity is the number of matching positions divided by the length of the alignment. Query cover is the percent of your query that took part in the alignment.

    Organism A  ATGATCCAGT
            | || ||||
Organism B  ACGACTCAGT     7 of 10 match → 70% identity

    DNA is only one line of evidence. Fossils, anatomy, embryology and protein sequences can all support or challenge a tree. A good cladogram is a hypothesis that fits the most evidence.

    What this simulation models
    • Process: Molecular evolution. DNA changes slowly over time, so species with a more recent common ancestor have more similar genes. A BLAST-style alignment finds and ranks the closest matches.
    • Included: Three genes evolved along a tree of real divergence dates, each at its own speed (slow, medium, fast), with faster change at third codon positions, some sites that never change, and rare insertions or deletions. Hits are scored as bit scores and E-values.
    • Simplified or left out: The sequences are simulated, not from GenBank, and the database holds about 20 species, not millions. Real fossil DNA this old would not survive. Real searches return messier, more surprising hits.
    Key terms
    Cladogram
    A branching diagram that shows a hypothesis of evolutionary relationships.
    Shared derived character
    A trait that arose in a common ancestor and is shared by all of its descendants.
    Homolog
    A gene or trait inherited from a common ancestor.
    Bit score
    A normalized alignment score that can be compared between searches; higher is closer.
    E-value
    The number of alignments this good expected by chance in a database of that size; lower is better.
    Percent identity
    The percentage of aligned positions where the two sequences match.

    3 · Observe the fossil and make a hypothesis

    Observations from the specimen:

    • A backbone runs into a long, bony tail made of many vertebrae.
    • The jaws hold small, sharp, curved teeth.
    • The forelimbs end in three clawed fingers. The thumb is not opposable.
    • Feather impressions line the arms, the legs and both sides of the tail.
    • The hind legs are long, and it stood on two legs.
    • A patch of soft tissue was preserved in the body cavity. This is where the DNA came from.
