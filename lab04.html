Investigation 4

Diffusion and Osmosis

What causes my plants to wilt if I forget to water them?

Unit 2: Cell Structure and FunctionENE-1.BENE-1.CENE-2.HScience Practices 4.A, 5.A≈ 4 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    A wilted houseplant perks up within an hour of watering. Water moves into its cells, the cells press against their walls, and the stems stiffen again. In this lab you will work out why water moves, which way it goes, and how the size and shape of a cell limit how fast anything can get in or out.

    The investigation has three procedures:

    1. Surface area and cell size. Soak agar "cells" that contain the pH indicator phenolphthalein in acid and watch the pink color disappear from the outside in. Measure how deep the acid gets and calculate what fraction of each block it reached.
    2. Modeling diffusion and osmosis. Fill dialysis-tubing bags with sucrose, salt, glucose, or protein solutions, soak them in a second solution, and use the change in mass to find the direction and rate of osmosis.
    3. Osmosis in living cells. Watch Elodea or onion cells plasmolyze under the microscope, then use potato cores in unlabeled sucrose solutions to find the water potential of plant tissue.

    Finally you will design your own experiment, using any of these systems.

    Safety in the real lab

    The 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions cause chemical burns. Wear goggles, gloves, and an apron, and keep the agar blocks and acid in a spill-proof tray. Your teacher prepares the concentrated solutions in a fume hood. Report every spill right away.

    Background

    Membranes, gradients, and water potential

    A cell has to take in nutrients and get rid of wastes across its plasma membrane, a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. The membrane is selectively permeable. Small nonpolar molecules slip through the lipid tails. Water crosses slowly through the bilayer and much faster through channel proteins called aquaporins. Ions and larger polar molecules such as sugars need channel or carrier proteins.

    Diffusion is the net movement of a substance from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated. It needs no energy from the cell; it happens because molecules are always moving randomly. Diffusion is faster when the temperature is higher (more kinetic energy), when the molecules are smaller, when the concentration gradient is steeper, and when the distance is shorter. At equilibrium molecules keep moving, but movement in each direction is equal, so there is no net change.

    Why cells are small

    Everything a cell exchanges has to pass through its surface, but the demand for exchange comes from its volume. As a cube grows, surface area grows with the square of the side length while volume grows with the cube, so the surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) falls. A large cell has too little membrane for its contents, and molecules diffusing inward take a long time to reach the center. Microvilli in the small intestine, root hairs, and the flattened shape of red blood cells all raise SA:V.

    cube: SA = 6s²  ·  V = s³  ·  SA:V = 6/s   |   box: SA = 2(LW + LH + WH)  ·  V = LWH

    Osmosis and tonicity

    Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Dissolved solutes hold water molecules in hydration shells, so a solution with more solute has less "free" water. Water therefore moves toward the side with more solute. Compared with another solution, a hypertonic solution has more solute, a hypotonic one has less, and isotonic solutions have equal water potential, so there is no net water movement. These words always compare two solutions; a solution is not hypertonic by itself.

    Water potential

    Plant cells have a rigid wall, so solute concentration alone does not predict water movement. Botanists use water potential (ψ, psi), measured in bars, which combines the effect of solutes and of pressure. Water always moves from higher ψ to lower ψ.

    ψ = ψP + ψS     ψS = −iCRT
    • ψS, solute potential: always zero or negative. i is the ionization constant (1 for sucrose or glucose, 2 for NaCl), C the molar concentration, R = 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K), and T the temperature in kelvins (273 + °C).
    • ψP, pressure potential: zero for a solution in an open beaker, positive (turgor pressure) inside a cell pushing on its wall, negative under tension in xylem.
    • Pure water in an open container has ψ = 0. Example: 0.15 M sucrose at 25 °C has ψS = −(1)(0.15)(0.0831)(298) = −3.7 bars; 0.15 M NaCl has twice that, −7.4 bars.

    When a plant cell sits in pure water, water flows in and the cell pushes on its wall until turgor pressure cancels the cell's negative solute potential. Then ψ inside equals ψ outside, and net movement stops. In a solution with lower ψ than the cell, water leaves, turgor drops to zero, and the membrane pulls away from the wall. This is plasmolysis, and it is why an unwatered plant wilts.
