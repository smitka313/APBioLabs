Cellular Respiration

What factors affect the rate of cellular respiration in multicellular organisms?

Unit 3: Cellular EnergeticsENE-1.KENE-1.LScience Practices 4.A, 6.B≈ 4 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    Seeds sitting in a dish do not look busy, but a germinating seed is burning through its stored food as fast as a small insect. In this lab you will measure that activity by tracking how much oxygen gas living things remove from a sealed chamber.

    You will build (virtually) a microrespirometer: a 1 mL syringe sealed to a thin capillary tube, with a drop of KOH to soak up carbon dioxide. As the seeds use O2, a tiny plug of colored fluid creeps down the capillary toward the seeds. You will mark its position every 5 minutes, correct your readings with a control respirometer full of glass beads, graph O2 consumed against time, and get the respiration rate from the slope. You will also try the electronic version (O2, CO2, or gas pressure sensors), and then design your own experiment on temperature, seed type, germination age, light, or small animals.

    Learning objectives
    • ENE-1.K Describe the processes that allow organisms to use energy stored in biological macromolecules.
    • ENE-1.L Explain how cells obtain energy from biological macromolecules in order to power cellular functions.
    Science practices
    • 4.A Construct a graph, plot, or chart (O2 consumed vs. time; rate vs. a variable).
    • 6.B Support a claim with evidence from biological principles, concepts, processes, and/or data.
    Safety in the real lab
    • Potassium hydroxide (KOH, or NaOH in drain cleaner) is caustic. Wear goggles, gloves, and an apron the whole time.
    • Use only one small drop of KOH, held in cotton. When you push the plunger in, point the capillary into a sink: extra KOH can squirt out.
    • Hot glue guns burn. Let the glue cool before you touch the capillary joint.
    • Work only with your teacher supervising.
    Background

    Turning gas volume into a respiration rate

    Every living cell needs a steady supply of free energy to build molecules, move materials, and stay organized. Plants capture energy through photosynthesis, but almost all of their cells, like ours, also have mitochondria and run cellular respiration to turn stored carbon compounds into ATP. A germinating seed cannot photosynthesize yet, so it lives entirely on the starch or oil packed into its cotyledons or endosperm.

    When enough oxygen is present, glucose is oxidized completely in a series of enzyme-catalyzed steps:

    C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + energy (≈ 686 kcal per mole of glucose)

    You could measure respiration by tracking any of the three things that change: O2 used, CO2 made, or heat released. Heat is hard to catch. Gas volumes are easier, but there is a catch: for glucose, one CO2 is released for every O2 consumed, so the total amount of gas in a sealed chamber barely changes. The trick is to remove the CO2 as soon as it forms. Potassium hydroxide does this by turning the gas into a solid:

    CO2 + 2 KOH → K2CO3 + H2O

    Now the only gas change left is the loss of O2, so the gas volume in the chamber shrinks. The shrinking gas pulls the fluid plug in the capillary toward the chamber.

    Gas volume also responds to things that have nothing to do with the seeds. The ideal gas law ties them together:

    PV = nRT   →   V = nRT / P

    Respiration lowers n (moles of gas), which lowers V. But if the water bath warms by a fraction of a degree, V goes up; if air pressure in the room rises, V goes down. That is why every experiment needs a control respirometer filled with non-living glass beads (or baked seeds) of the same volume. It sits in the same bath, so any movement of its plug comes only from temperature and pressure changes. Subtracting the control's movement from the seeds' movement leaves the part caused by respiration.

    To turn plug movement into a gas volume, treat the inside of the capillary as a cylinder. Our capillaries have an inner radius of 0.040 cm, so each centimeter of travel is

    V = πr²h = π(0.040 cm)²(1 cm) = 0.00503 cm³ = 5.03 µL of O2

    Plot O2 consumed (µL) on the y-axis against time (min) on the x-axis. The slope of the best-fit line is the respiration rate in µL O2/min. Dividing by the mass of the organisms gives a rate per gram, which lets you compare organisms of different sizes.

    Respiration is a chain of enzyme reactions, so it speeds up with temperature until the enzymes start to denature. Biologists summarize the temperature effect with the Q10, the factor by which a rate increases for a 10 °C rise. Most respiration has a Q10 between 2 and 3:

    Q10 = (R2 / R1)10 / (T2 − T1)

    Procedure · Option 1 · Structured inquiry

    Measure respiration with microrespirometers

    How the real microrespirometers are built and charged
    1. Push a 40 µL plastic capillary into the tip of a 1 mL tuberculin syringe (no needle) as far as the plunger tip, and seal the joint with hot glue. Check that the glue did not plug the capillary.
    2. Draw soapy red manometer fluid through the capillary and push it out again. The soap film stops the plug from sticking.
    3. Pack a small plug of absorbent cotton to the 0 mL end of the barrel, add one small drop of 15% KOH, then add a plug of non-absorbent cotton on top to protect the seeds.
    4. Push the plunger in (capillary pointed into a sink) to remove excess KOH, then remove the plunger.
    5. Add 0.5 mL of germinating seeds and push the plunger to the 1.0 mL mark. Build a second, identical respirometer with 0.5 mL of glass beads as the control.
    6. Slide 3–4 washers onto each barrel, set both in a 20 °C water bath with the barrels under water and the capillaries sticking out, and wait 5 minutes for temperatures to even out.
    7. Add one drop of manometer fluid to each capillary tip. It is pulled in and seals the chamber. Mark the bottom edge of each plug: this is time 0. Mark again every 5 minutes for 25 minutes.

    Designing and conducting your investigation

    Your own respiration experiment

    Now you have a tool. Pick one question and test it. Some questions from the manual:

    • Does temperature change the rate of respiration in germinating seeds? Do seeds use more O2 when it is warmer?
    • Do germinating seeds respire faster than non-germinating seeds? Do seeds just starting to germinate respire faster than seeds that have been germinating for several days?
    • Do oil-storing seeds (Wisconsin Fast Plants) respire at a different rate from starch-storing grass seeds (oats)? Monocots vs. dicots?
    • Can the same respirometer measure respiration in small invertebrates like mealworms, crickets, fruit flies, or earthworms? How do they compare per gram?
    • What happens if you use green seedlings in the light instead of seeds in the dark?
