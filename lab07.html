Cell Division: Mitosis and Meiosis

How do eukaryotic cells divide to produce genetically identical cells or to produce gametes with half the normal DNA?

Unit 4: Cell CycleUnit 5: HeredityIST-1.BIST-1.CIST-1.FIST-1.GScience Practices 1.B, 4.B, 5.A≈ 4–5 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    Every one of your trillions of cells came from a single zygote, and every egg or sperm you make carries exactly half of your chromosomes. In this lab you will model how chromosomes move in mitosis and meiosis, count dividing cells in onion root tips to test whether a fungal lectin speeds up mitosis, compare the chromosomes of normal and cancer cells, and use the fungus Sordaria fimicola to measure how often crossing over happens.

    The investigation has five parts:

    1. Modeling mitosis with pop-bead chromosomes, then putting real cell images in order.
    2. Effects of environment on mitosis: score onion root-tip squashes from control and lectin-treated bulbs, pool class data, and run a chi-square test.
    3. Loss of cell cycle control in cancer: count and compare chromosomes in normal, HeLa, and leukemia karyotypes.
    4. Modeling meiosis with crossing over, independent assortment, and nondisjunction.
    5. Crossing over in Sordaria: score asci and calculate the gene-to-centromere map distance.
    Learning objectives
    • IST-1.B Describe the events that occur in the cell cycle.
    • IST-1.C Explain how mitosis results in the transmission of chromosomes from one generation to the next.
    • IST-1.F Explain how meiosis results in the transmission of chromosomes from one generation to the next.
    • IST-1.G Describe similarities and differences between the phases and outcomes of mitosis and meiosis.

    Science practices: 1.B explain biological concepts and processes · 4.B describe data from a table or graph · 5.A perform mathematical calculations (mitotic index, chi-square, map distance).

    Safety in the real lab

    Root-tip squashes use 1 M hydrochloric acid, Carnoy's fixative (glacial acetic acid and ethanol), and carbol-fuchsin stain, which contains phenol. Wear goggles and disposable gloves for Parts 2 and 5. Use a razor blade with care, and press cover slips with a pencil eraser through a lab wipe, never your thumb. Purified lectin can irritate skin and airways. Dispose of chemicals as your teacher directs.

    Background

    The cell cycle, its controls, and two kinds of division

    Key ideas
    • The cell cycle is G1, S (DNA replication), G2 and M (mitosis plus cytokinesis). Interphase is G1 + S + G2 and takes most of the time.
    • Mitosis passes one full copy of every chromosome to each daughter, so the two cells are identical and keep 2n.
    • Cyclins, CDKs and three checkpoints control the cycle; cancer cells have lost this control.
    • Meiosis has two divisions after one S phase and makes four different haploid cells, through crossing over and independent assortment.
    • Mitotic index = dividing cells ÷ cells counted. Map distance = % crossover asci ÷ 2.

    Bacteria and archaea divide by binary fission, and so do mitochondria and chloroplasts, which is one line of evidence that these organelles descended from free-living prokaryotes. Eukaryotic cells have a harder job: they must copy many linear chromosomes, sort the copies precisely, and split a large cytoplasm full of organelles. The sequence of events that does this is the cell cycle.

    Interphase takes up most of the cycle. In G1 the cell grows and makes organelles. In S phase every chromosome is replicated, so each chromosome now consists of two identical sister chromatids held together at the centromere. In G2 the cell checks the new DNA and prepares to divide. A G2 cell has twice the DNA of a G1 cell but no new genetic information, because the sisters are copies. Cells that stop dividing leave the cycle and sit in G0.

    In mitosis the condensed chromosomes line up at the metaphase plate, the sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles by spindle microtubules attached at their kinetochores, and two nuclei form. Cytokinesis then splits the cytoplasm (a furrow in animal cells, a cell plate in plant cells). Condensing first matters: long, tangled interphase DNA would snag and break if it were dragged across the cell, like trying to separate cooked spaghetti, whereas compact chromosomes move like pieces of macaroni.

    Cyclins, CDKs, and checkpoints

    Progress through the cycle is driven by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), enzymes that are active only when bound to a cyclin. Cyclin levels rise and fall each cycle. When enough mitotic cyclin accumulates, it binds CDK to form MPF (mitosis-promoting factor), which phosphorylates proteins that launch mitosis; cyclin is then destroyed and MPF activity crashes so the cell can finish dividing.

    Three checkpoints stop the cycle if something is wrong. The G1 checkpoint asks whether the cell is big enough and has growth signals such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF). The G2 checkpoint asks whether DNA replication is complete and undamaged. The M-spindle checkpoint holds anaphase until every kinetochore is attached to spindle microtubules. Tumor-suppressor proteins such as p53 and Rb enforce these stops; when they are mutated, damaged cells keep dividing, which is a hallmark of cancer.

    Mitosis versus meiosis

    Meiosis is a reduction division. After one round of DNA replication the cell divides twice, turning one diploid (2n) cell into four haploid (n) cells. In prophase I homologous chromosomes pair and non-sister chromatids exchange segments by crossing over. In metaphase I each homologous pair lines up independently of the others (independent assortment), and in anaphase I the homologs, not the sisters, separate. Meiosis II then separates sister chromatids. If homologs or sisters fail to separate (nondisjunction), gametes end up with an extra or a missing chromosome.

    Two numbers you'll calculate
    mitotic index = (cells in prophase + metaphase + anaphase + telophase) ÷ total cells counted
    gene–centromere distance (map units) = (% asci showing crossover) ÷ 2

    The mitotic index estimates the fraction of a tissue's cells that are dividing at the moment it was fixed. One map unit equals 1% recombinant products. In a Sordaria ascus with a crossover, only half of the spores carry recombinant chromatids, which is why the percentage is divided by 2.

    Key terms
    Sister chromatids
    The two identical copies of a chromosome made in S phase, joined at the centromere.
    Checkpoint
    A control point that halts the cycle if DNA is damaged or chromosomes are not attached.
    Cyclin and CDK
    Cyclin switches on its kinase (CDK); the pair is MPF, which drives mitosis.
    Mitotic index
    The fraction of cells in prophase, metaphase, anaphase or telophase when fixed.
    Homologous chromosomes
    A maternal and a paternal chromosome carrying the same genes; they pair in meiosis I.
    Crossing over
    Exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of a tetrad in prophase I.
    Map unit
    1% recombinant products; here, % crossover asci ÷ 2.

    Part 2 · Effects of environment on mitosis

    Does a fungal lectin speed up mitosis in root tips?

    Soybean crops did poorly during three wet years, and their roots were stunted. A lectin-like protein, possibly secreted by a soil fungus, was found around the roots. Lectins are proteins that bind specific carbohydrates, and some act as mitogens that push cells into mitosis; tissue that divides too fast can be weak. You will test the idea with onion bulbs, which grow roots more easily than soybeans. One jar of bulbs grew in sand wet with water (control); the other grew in sand wet with lectin solution (PHA-M, 10 mg in 200 mL).

    Chi-square test

    Null hypothesis: the lectin has no effect, so treated roots have the same proportion of cells in mitosis as control roots. There are two ways to get expected values; both have df = 1 and a critical value of 3.84 at p = 0.05.

    Part 5 · Meiosis and crossing over in Sordaria

    How far is the tan gene from its centromere?

    Sordaria fimicola is a fungus whose haploid hyphae fuse where two strains meet on a plate. The brief diploid zygote undergoes meiosis and then one mitosis inside a sac called an ascus, giving eight spores lined up in the order the nuclei divided. You crossed wild type (+, black spores) with tan (tn). Without a crossover between the spore-color gene and the centromere, the alleles separate in meiosis I and the ascus shows a 4:4 pattern. A crossover delays separation to meiosis II, giving 2:2:2:2 or 2:4:2.
