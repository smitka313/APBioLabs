Biotechnology: Bacterial Transformation

How can we use genetic engineering techniques to manipulate heritable information?

Unit 6: Gene Expression and RegulationIST-1.PScience Practice 6.D≈ 4 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    You will move a jellyfish gene into E. coli. Using the pGLO plasmid, you'll make bacteria competent with calcium chloride, heat-shock them, let them recover, and spread them on three kinds of agar plates. After incubation you'll find out which cells picked up the plasmid, which ones glow green, and how efficient your transformation was.

    In the virtual bench below you carry out each step of the real protocol. You can follow it exactly, or change a step (skip the ice, shorten the heat shock, add more plasmid) and see how the outcome changes. You'll predict every plate before you see it, count colonies, and calculate transformation efficiency the same way the manual does. Then you'll design your own experiment on what makes transformation work.

    Learning objectives
    • IST-1.P Explain the use of genetic engineering techniques in analyzing or manipulating DNA.
    • Science Practice 6.D Explain the relationship between experimental results and larger biological concepts, processes, or theories.
    • Use selection (ampicillin) and an inducible gene (GFP under arabinose control) to tell transformed cells from untransformed cells.
    • Calculate transformation efficiency and report it in scientific notation.
    Safety at the real bench
    • Use sterile technique. Do not touch the loop ring, pipette tips, or agar surface, and never set them on the bench.
    • The E. coli strain is not pathogenic, but treat it with care: wear goggles and gloves, wash your hands before and after, and keep food, drinks, cosmetics, and phones out of the work area.
    • Wipe down work surfaces with 10% bleach. Soak all used tubes, loops, and plates in 10% bleach for 20 minutes (or autoclave) before disposal.
    • Ampicillin is in the penicillin family. If you are allergic to penicillin, avoid contact with it. Rinse eyes with water if exposed.
    • Keep your nose and mouth away from the tip when pipetting cell suspensions.
    Background

    Moving genes into bacteria

    Information in DNA directs a cell through RNA and protein. Change a cell's DNA and you can change what it makes and how it behaves. Biotechnologists use this to make bacteria that produce human insulin, vaccine proteins, and industrial enzymes. The same tools raise real questions, such as whether genetically modified crops like Bt corn are safe to eat.

    Bacteria gain new genes in three natural ways: conjugation (direct cell-to-cell transfer), transduction (a virus carries DNA in), and transformation (a cell takes up "naked" DNA from its surroundings). Frederick Griffith first saw transformation in 1928, when harmless pneumonia bacteria became deadly after mixing with heat-killed deadly ones.

    Most lab transformations use plasmids: small, circular DNA molecules that copy themselves separately from the bacterial chromosome. A plasmid needs an origin of replication (ori) and usually carries a few useful genes. The plasmid you'll use, pGLO, carries three genes that matter for this lab:

    • bla codes for beta-lactamase, an enzyme the cell secretes that breaks down ampicillin. Cells with pGLO survive on ampicillin; cells without it die.
    • GFP codes for green fluorescent protein from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria. It glows green under UV light.
    • araC codes for a regulatory protein. Without the sugar arabinose, AraC keeps the GFP gene switched off. When arabinose binds AraC, the protein changes shape and turns on transcription of GFP. This is an inducible operon, similar to the lac operon.

    Why the procedure works. DNA and the cell's outer membrane are both negatively charged, so they repel each other. Calcium ions (Ca2+) in the cold transformation solution neutralize those charges and let plasmids stick to the cell surface. Chilling makes the membrane rigid. A sudden heat shock (0 °C → 42 °C → 0 °C) creates a temperature imbalance across the membrane that helps pull plasmids inside. The cells then recover in LB broth so they can repair their membranes and start making beta-lactamase before they meet ampicillin.

    How you know it worked. Each colony on an agar plate grows from one cell. Only transformed cells can grow on LB/amp, so counting colonies there counts transformation events. On LB/amp/ara plates, transformed colonies also glow. Plates without ampicillin are controls that show the cells were alive.

    Transformation efficiency tells you how many cells were transformed per microgram of plasmid that reached the plate:

    Transformation efficiency = colonies on the LB/amp plate ÷ µg of plasmid DNA spread on that plate
    DNA used (µg) = concentration (µg/µL) × volume (µL)
    Fraction spread = volume spread (µL) ÷ total volume in tube (µL)
    DNA spread (µg) = DNA used × fraction spread

    Efficiencies are large numbers, so report them in scientific notation, for example 3.1 × 103 transformants/µg.
