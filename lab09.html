Biotechnology: Restriction Enzyme Analysis of DNA

How can we use genetic information to identify and profile individuals?

Unit 6: Gene Expression and RegulationIST-1.PScience Practice 6.D≈ 2 class periods + gel run
    Overview

    The case of the missing teacher

    You arrive at AP Biology, Room 102. Chairs are knocked over, there is broken glass on the floor, there are drops of blood, and Ms. Mason is nowhere to be found. Your classmates Marcus and Laurel bag the evidence. Your job is to use DNA to find out whose blood is on the floor.

    Evidence log
    • Ten small drops on the floor, confirmed as blood by a Kastle-Meyer test
    • A shard from a broken 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask with a reddish stain on the edge
    • A paper cup with lipstick, presumed to be Ms. Mason's (saliva)
    • A wad of bubble gum under an overturned chair (Bobby chews that brand)
    • A tissue Mr. Gladson, the teacher next door, threw in the hall trash
    • Bobby's photosynthesis test with a large red "F"
    • An email from Mr. Gladson asking Ms. Mason to give up her job as department chair

    You will:

    1. Use restriction enzymes to cut DNA on paper, and predict the fragments from a map of lambda (λ) phage DNA.
    2. Cast, load and run a virtual agarose gel with uncut λ DNA and λ DNA cut with EcoRI, HindIII and BamHI.
    3. Measure how far each band moved, build a semi-log standard curve from the HindIII marker, and estimate the sizes of unknown fragments.
    4. Design and run your own gel of the crime-scene evidence. Then use your numbers, not just your eyes, to decide whose blood it is.
    Learning objective and practice
    • IST-1.P Explain the use of genetic engineering techniques in analyzing or manipulating DNA.
    • Science Practice 6.D Explain the relationship between experimental results and larger biological concepts, processes, or theories.
    Safety at the real bench
    • The gel box holds a salt buffer and uses DC current strong enough to shock you. Turn the power supply OFF and wait 10 seconds before touching any lead.
    • Connect black to black and red to red, then switch the power on. Power goes on last and off first.
    • Wear gloves. Never handle gels with bare hands, and keep methylene blue stain off your skin.
    • Hot agarose can boil over and scald you. Use heat-resistant gloves when you melt it.
    Background

    Cutting DNA and sorting it by size

    Restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases) are proteins bacteria use to chop up the DNA of invading viruses. Each one recognizes a specific short sequence, usually a 4- to 8-base-pair palindrome, which means the sequence reads the same 5′→3′ on both strands. The enzyme cuts the sugar–phosphate backbone at that site. EcoRI (from E. coli) recognizes 5′-GAATTC-3′ and cuts between G and A on each strand. Because the two cuts are staggered, each piece ends with a four-base single-stranded overhang, called a sticky end. Other enzymes, such as SmaI (CCC↓GGG), cut straight across and leave blunt ends. Fragments with matching sticky ends can base-pair and be sealed by DNA ligase. This "cut and paste" step is the basis of recombinant DNA.

    Cutting a DNA molecule with an enzyme gives a set of fragments whose sizes depend on where the sites are. Differences between individuals' DNA can add or remove sites, or change the distance between them. This gives each person a different set of restriction fragment length polymorphisms (RFLPs). That pattern is a DNA "fingerprint." Identical twins have the same pattern.

    Gel electrophoresis separates the fragments. DNA's phosphate groups give it a negative charge, so in an electric field it moves from the negative (black) electrode toward the positive (red) electrode. That is why the wells go at the black end. The agarose gel is a mesh of pores. Short fragments slip through quickly, while long ones get tangled and move slowly. Over a useful range, the distance a fragment travels is roughly a straight-line function of the logarithm of its size:

    distance ≈ a − b · log10(size in bp)   ⇔   log10(bp) = m · distance + c

    To size unknown fragments, run a DNA marker (a set of fragments of known size) on the same gel. Here the marker is λ DNA cut with HindIII: 23,130, 9,416, 6,557, 4,361, 2,322, 2,027, 564 and 125 bp. Plot distance on the ordinary x-axis and size on the logarithmic y-axis of semi-log paper. The points fall close to a straight line, called the standard curve. Read an unknown band's distance across to the line to estimate its size. The very largest fragments squeeze together near the top (above about 20 kb at 0.8% agarose) and fall off the line, so leave them out of the fit. λ DNA has 12-base single-stranded "cos" ends that can stick together. In a HindIII digest, the two end fragments (23,130 + 4,361) partly pair up into a 27,491-bp piece that runs in the same band as 23,130.

    Loading dye contains a dense sugar that makes the sample sink into the well. It also contains a blue-purple dye that runs at about the same speed as a 300-bp DNA fragment, so you can see how far the run has gone. Agarose concentration, voltage and run time all change how well the bands separate.

    Procedure · Learning to use gel electrophoresis
    Load and run λ DNA digests
    
    
    
      
    Your gel (0.8% agarose in 1× TAE) is cast, set in the box with the comb at the black (−) end, covered with buffer, and the comb has been removed. Load each sample into its own well: pick a tube, set the volume, then click a well, either on the gel or with the well buttons. Record your loading order. Connect the leads, run the gel until the dye front is about 1 cm from the end, then stain it. The manual's "ideal gel" order is A BamHI, B EcoRI, C HindIII, D uncut.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    
    Analyzing results · Sizing fragments
    Measure bands and build a standard curve
    
    
    
      
    After staining, click a band, or use "Measure all visible bands in lane." Each click adds the distance from the front of the well to the front edge of the band. Readings carry about ±0.5 mm of ruler error, as at the bench. In the Known size column, type the size of each HindIII marker band, largest (closest to the well) first: 23,130 (the 23,130/27,491 doublet), 9,416, 6,557, 4,361, 2,322, 2,027 … The page fits log10(bp) against distance for each gel, then estimates every band with no known size.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    
    Designing and conducting your investigation
    Whose blood is on the floor?
    
    
    
      
    The chief investigator (your teacher) gives you DNA from the blood, Ms. Mason (saliva on her cup), Mr. Gladson (his tissue) and Bobby (bubble gum). All four have been cut with the same restriction enzymes, and you also get a HindIII marker. Plan your gel: which wells, how much sample, which voltage, how long, and how many repeat gels. Then run it and size every band. A jury will not accept "the bands look the same." You need fragment sizes and a clear match rule.
