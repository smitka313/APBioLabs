Investigation 10

Energy Dynamics

What factors govern energy capture, allocation, storage, and transfer between producers and consumers in a terrestrial ecosystem?

Unit 8: EcologyENE-1.MENE-1.NENE-1.OSYI-3.AScience Practices 6.C, 6.D≈ 2–3 weeks of short sessions
    Overview

    Running an energy budget for a tiny ecosystem

    You will build a two-species model ecosystem: Wisconsin Fast Plants (Brassica rapa) as the producer and cabbage white butterfly larvae (Pieris rapae) as the consumer. By tracking biomass and converting it to kilocalories, you will account for energy the way an accountant tracks money: what comes in, what is stored, and what is spent.

    In the real lab these steps take two to three weeks. Here the plants grow in a few clicks, but you still have to make the measurements, do the calculations, and decide what your numbers mean.

    What you will do
    • Part 1: Harvest Fast Plants at two ages, dry them, and use dry mass (biomass) to estimate net primary productivity (NPP). Then measure plant respiration to estimate gross primary productivity (GPP).
    • Part 2: Feed 4th-instar larvae a Brussels sprout for 3 days. Measure what they eat, how much they grow, and how much frass (waste) they produce, then audit where the energy went.
    • Part 3: Design your own investigation of light, temperature, crowding, fertilizer, or food type.
    Learning objectives
    • ENE-1.M Describe the strategies organisms use to acquire and use energy.
    • ENE-1.N Explain how changes in energy availability affect populations and ecosystems.
    • ENE-1.O Explain how the activities of autotrophs and heterotrophs enable the flow of energy within an ecosystem.
    • SYI-3.A Connect variation in the molecules within organisms to their ability to survive and reproduce in different environments.
    • Science practices 6.C, 6.D Justify claims with evidence and connect results to larger biological concepts.
    Safety and care of organisms
    • The USDA lists cabbage white butterflies as a pest. Never release larvae or adults outdoors. When the work is done, larvae and butterflies are euthanized by freezing; plants and soil can be thrown away.
    • Never put research organisms under extreme stress, such as high temperatures. That is why the design options below limit larval temperatures to a humane range.
    • Keep growing chambers clean (wipe with dilute bleach, then dry) to avoid disease outbreaks, and keep water away from the light fixtures.
    Background

    Biomass is how we count energy

    Almost every food web runs on sunlight. During photosynthesis, producers capture light energy and store it in the chemical bonds of sugars and other organic molecules. Those molecules become the plant's body: its biomass. The total rate at which producers capture energy is gross primary productivity (GPP). Plants spend part of that energy right away on their own cellular respiration (R). What is left over and stored as new tissue is net primary productivity (NPP).

    NPP = GPP − Rplant

    We cannot count kilocalories directly, but organic molecules have mass, so we can measure biomass and convert. The catch is water. A living Fast Plant is mostly water, and water stores no usable chemical energy. That is why ecologists dry organisms in an oven (about 100 °C for 48 hours) and weigh the dry mass. The dry-to-wet ratio (the "percent biomass") lets you estimate the biomass of living things you do not want to kill, such as a Brussels sprout you are about to feed to larvae.

    energy (kcal) = wet mass (g) × (dry ÷ wet) × energy per gram of dry mass

    Different molecules hold different amounts of energy: about 4 kcal per gram for carbohydrates and proteins, about 9 kcal per gram for fats. In this lab you will use measured averages from bomb calorimetry: 4.35 kcal/g for dry plant biomass, 5.5 kcal/g for dry larval biomass (larvae store more fat), and 4.76 kcal/g for dry frass.

    When a larva eats, the energy it consumes (C) splits three ways. Some passes through the gut undigested and leaves as frass, which is egested energy (F). The rest is assimilated (A) into the body. Of the assimilated energy, some builds new tissue (production, P) and the rest is used in cellular respiration and lost as heat (R). Because energy is neither created nor destroyed, the budget must balance:

    C = P + R + F   →   R = C − P − F

    Respiration is hard to measure directly in a caterpillar, so you will estimate it as the energy left over after the other terms are measured. Every conversion loses energy as heat, which is the second law of thermodynamics at work. That is why only a small fraction of the energy at one trophic level shows up as biomass in the next.

    From NPP to GPP

    NPP between your two harvests is the biomass the average plant added per day, converted to kcal. To estimate GPP you also need the energy the plants spent on respiration. Seal 5 living plants in a dark 2.0 L chamber with a CO2 sensor for 10 minutes (no photosynthesis in the dark, so the CO2 rise is respiration only). Do this on the same day as a harvest so you know the plants' mean dry mass.

    1 ppm CO2 in 2.0 L of air ≈ 0.00364 mg CO2  ·  1 g CO2 respired ≈ 0.68 g biomass ≈ 2.97 kcal

    Procedure 2 · Structured inquiry

    Energy flow from producer to consumer

    Weigh one large fresh Brussels sprout, cut it in half, and place both halves in a clean Brassica Barn. Weigh a group of 8–12 fourth-instar larvae, add them, and let them feed for 3 days. Each day, the frass (droppings) falls onto a paper-towel pad; you collect it, dry it, and weigh it. After 3 days, weigh the larvae again and weigh what is left of the sprout.

    A cut sprout also loses water to evaporation, which has nothing to do with eating. A second sprout in an identical barn with no larvae (a control) lets you correct for that.

    Each run is one barn. Run at least 3 barns so you can calculate standard error.

    The student manual suggests assuming larvae are 40% dry mass and using your Fast Plant percentage for sprouts. Both are assumptions you can test.
