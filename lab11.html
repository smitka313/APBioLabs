Investigation 11

Transpiration

What factors, including environmental variables, affect the rate of transpiration in plants?

Unit 8: EcologyENE-3.DIST-5.ASYI-1.HScience Practices 3.C, 5.A≈ 4 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    A single tomato plant can move more than a liter of water from the soil to the air on a hot day, and it keeps less than 5% of that water. Why lose so much? And what decides how fast it goes?

    In Part 1 you will learn two measuring skills: finding leaf surface area by tracing leaves on a grid, and counting stomata in a clear nail-polish peel under the microscope to find stomata per mm². In Part 2 you will measure how fast whole plants or cuttings lose water, with a potometer, the whole-plant mass method, or a gas pressure sensor, and compare a control with a fan, a lamp, or a humid misted bag. Then you will design your own experiment on an environmental factor and test it with replicates and statistics.

    Learning objectives
    • ENE-3.D Explain how the behavioral and/or physiological response of an organism is related to changes in its internal or external environment.
    • IST-5.A Explain how the behavioral responses of organisms affect their overall fitness and may contribute to the success of the population.
    • SYI-1.H Explain how the density of a population affects and is determined by resource availability in the environment.
    Science practices
    • 3.C Identify experimental procedures that are aligned to the question.
    • 5.A Perform mathematical calculations (areas, densities, rates per unit area, means and standard errors).
    Safety in the real lab
    • Nail polish is toxic if swallowed or inhaled and irritates eyes. Use it in a ventilated room and keep it away from your face.
    • Cut stems with a razor blade or scalpel slowly, away from your fingers, at a 45° angle. Cutting under water keeps air out of the xylem.
    • Wear goggles while running experiments. Heat lamps get hot: keep them away from paper, plastic bags, and water.
    • If you fill tubing with a syringe, make sure the needle has been removed.
    Background

    How water gets pulled up a plant

    Key ideas
    • Transpiration is the evaporation of water from leaves, mostly through stomata. A plant keeps only a small fraction of the water it takes up.
    • Water moves from higher to lower water potential (ψ), and the dry air around a leaf has a very low ψ.
    • TACT (transpiration, adhesion, cohesion, tension) pulls water up the xylem with no pump.
    • Stomata trade water loss against CO2 uptake; guard cells open and close the pore.
    • E = g × VPD / P: warm, dry, windy, bright conditions raise the rate; humid air lowers it.

    Plants absorb water and dissolved ions from the soil through root hairs by osmosis, diffusion, and active transport. Once water reaches the root xylem, it travels up through stems to the leaves. Most of it then evaporates out of the leaves: this loss of water vapor from the plant surface is transpiration. A plant uses only a small fraction of the water it takes up for growth and photosynthesis; the rest is transpired.

    Water always moves from higher water potential (ψ, more free energy) to lower water potential. Soil water is near 0 MPa, leaf cells are more negative, and dry air is extremely negative (around −100 MPa at 50% humidity). That gradient is what drives the whole system.

    ψ = ψP + ψS    ψsoil > ψroot > ψstem > ψleaf > ψair
    TACT: how water climbs a tree

    The TACT mechanism (Transpiration, Adhesion, Cohesion, Tension) explains how water climbs even a 100-foot tree with no pump. Water evaporates from the wet walls of mesophyll cells into air spaces inside the leaf (transpiration). This pulls on the water film behind it, creating tension (negative pressure). Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules to each other (cohesion), so the pull is passed down an unbroken column all the way to the roots, while water sticks to the narrow xylem walls (adhesion), which helps keep the column from breaking. An air bubble breaks the column, which is why you must keep air out of a potometer and cut stems under water.

    Stomata and the leaf cross-section

    Water vapor leaves mainly through stomata, pores flanked by two guard cells. Stomata must open to let in CO2 for photosynthesis (and let out O2), so every plant faces a trade-off between gaining carbon and losing water. Guard cells open the pore when they take up K+ and water and swell; they close it in the dark or when the hormone abscisic acid signals drought.

    A model for the transpiration rate: E = g × VPD / P

    A simple physical model ties the environmental factors together. The rate of transpiration per unit leaf area (E) is a conductance times a driving force:

    E = g × VPD / P     1/g = 1/gstomata + 1/gboundary layer

    The driving force is the vapor pressure deficit (VPD): how much drier the air is than the saturated air inside the leaf. Warm, dry air means a big VPD; a humid, misted bag shrinks it toward zero. Water vapor must also cross two "resistances" in series: the stomata (set by how many there are and how wide they open, which depends on light and on the plant's water status) and the thin boundary layer of still, humid air hugging the leaf. Wind strips the boundary layer away, so a fan raises transpiration. Because E is a rate per unit area, you must measure leaf surface area to compare plants of different sizes.

    Key terms
    Transpiration
    Loss of water vapor from the surfaces of a plant, mostly through stomata.
    Water potential (ψ)
    Free energy of water; water flows from higher to lower ψ.
    TACT
    Transpiration, adhesion, cohesion, tension: the mechanism that lifts water up the xylem.
    Stoma / guard cells
    A pore and the two cells that open and close it.
    VPD
    Vapor pressure deficit: how much drier the air is than the saturated air inside the leaf.
    Boundary layer
    A thin layer of still, humid air on the leaf surface; wind strips it away.

    Full procedure

    Click or tap each stoma (a pair of bean-shaped guard cells) to tally it. Click again to un-mark. Then record the field.

    How to make the peel (real lab)
    1. Paint a patch of clear nail polish at least 1 cm² on the leaf surface you are studying. Let it dry completely.
    2. Press clear cellophane tape onto the dried polish and peel it off gently by a corner. This is your leaf impression.
    3. Tape the impression to a clean slide, trim the extra tape, and label the species and the leaf side (upper or lower).
    4. Scan at low power, then count all stomata in one field at 400X. Count at least three more separate fields. Low contrast? Close the condenser diaphragm a little; no stain is needed.
    5. Trade slides with two other teams and count theirs the same way.

    Part 2 · Guided inquiry

    Measure the rate of transpiration

    Predict first

    Before you run anything: how will each condition (fan, heat lamp, mist + bag, dark) change the transpiration rate compared with the control? Rank them from the biggest increase to the biggest decrease and give a reason (1 to 2 sentences).

    Controls

    More options

    Each plant is a different individual, so expect natural variation. The animation shows the last plant in the batch.

    Full procedure

    Choose a method, then run the class's agreed control and at least one environmental treatment, with several plants each. For every plant, divide the rate by its leaf area (from the grid-tracing method) so that big and small plants can be compared. Then subtract the control mean to get the adjusted rate.

    • Potometer: a fresh cutting (cut under water at 45°) sealed into water-filled tubing connected to a 1.0 mL pipette. As the plant transpires, it pulls water out of the pipette. Read the pipette every 3 min for 30 min after a 10-min equilibration.
    • Whole-plant method: a well-watered potted plant with its pot and soil sealed in a plastic bag so only leaves lose water. Weigh it once a day for 4 days.
    • Gas pressure sensor: the tubing connects to a pressure sensor instead of a pipette. As water is pulled into the plant, pressure in the sealed tube falls (kPa).
