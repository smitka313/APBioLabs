Fruit Fly Behavior

What environmental factors trigger a fruit fly response?

Unit 8: EcologyENE-3.DIST-5.ASYI-1.HScience Practices 3.C, 5.A≈ 2–4 class periods
    Overview

    What you'll investigate

    Fruit flies find food and places to lay eggs by responding to cues in their environment. In this lab you'll put 20–40 flies into a choice chamber made from two plastic bottles, give them a different stimulus at each end, and count where they go. A chi-square test will tell you whether the flies really prefer one end or are just spread out by chance.

    First you'll learn to tell male and female flies apart and review the fly life cycle. Then you'll watch flies respond to gravity in a vial, run a structured set of choice-chamber trials with household substances, and finally design your own investigation of chemotaxis, phototaxis, or geotaxis.

    • SYI-1.H Explain how the density of a population affects and is determined by resource availability in the environment.

    Safety at the real bench
    • Only add substances your teacher has approved, in small amounts (5–10 drops on a cotton ball).
    • Ethanol is flammable. Keep it away from flames and heat sources. Wear goggles when handling liquids.
    • Many test substances are foods. Do not eat any of them.
    • Never release flies outdoors. When you finish, tap them through a funnel into the "morgue" (a beaker of salad oil or 70% alcohol).
    Background

    The dew-loving, black-bellied fly

    Drosophila melanogaster has been a model organism since Thomas Hunt Morgan began breeding it in 1907. It is small, cheap to raise, has a short generation time, and its genome is fully sequenced. The genus name means "lover of dew," because adults tend to emerge from their pupal cases in the early morning. The species name means "black belly," after the dark abdomen of the males. In the wild it feeds on fruit and on the yeasts and other fungi that grow on fruit as it rots.

    Taxis and kinesis

    Animals move in response to stimuli. A taxis is oriented movement: the animal turns and moves directly toward (positive taxis) or away from (negative taxis) a stimulus. A chemotaxis is a response to a chemical, a phototaxis a response to light, and a geotaxis a response to gravity. Adult fruit flies show a negative geotaxis: they climb upward. Adults also move toward bright light, while larvae move away from it.

    A kinesis is not oriented. The stimulus changes how fast an animal moves or how often it turns, but not its direction. Animals that slow down in good conditions and speed up in bad ones end up collecting in the good areas just by chance. Both mechanisms can put animals in the same place, so you have to watch how they get there.

    Is the pattern real? The chi-square test

    Even flies that don't care will rarely split exactly 15:15. The chi-square (χ²) test asks how likely your counts would be if the null hypothesis were true. For a choice chamber the null hypothesis is "flies have no preference," so the expected count at each end is half the flies.

    χ² = Σ (o − e)² ⁄ e    degrees of freedom = (number of categories) − 1 = 1

    With 1 degree of freedom, the critical value at p = 0.05 is 3.84. If χ² is greater than 3.84, a difference this large would happen by chance less than 5% of the time, so you reject the null hypothesis and conclude the flies show a preference. If χ² is smaller, you fail to reject the null hypothesis: your data do not show a preference.

    Pre-lab · Getting started

    Male or female?

    Look at the abdomen. Females are larger, with a longer, pointed abdomen that has separate dark stripes all the way to a light tip. Males are smaller, with a rounded abdomen whose last segments are fused into one solid dark patch, and they have tiny dark sex combs on their front legs. Use the reference, then identify the flies.

    Procedure · Structured inquiry

    The choice chamber

    Two bottles with their bottoms cut off are taped together; end A and end B each have a cap holding a cotton ball with 5–10 drops of a test substance. Follow the manual's steps:

    1. Start with distilled water at both ends, lay the chamber flat, and count after 5 minutes. This control tells you whether something other than your substances (light from a window, a tilt) is moving the flies.
    2. Predict what the flies will prefer, then test at least four substances, each against distilled water. Change caps between substances.
    3. Rotate the chamber (Swap ends) and repeat to check that position is not the cause.
    4. Count flies at each end at each time point, then use chi-square to test the final counts.

    Designing and conducting your investigation

    Your own fruit fly experiment

    Now that you've seen how flies respond to single substances, ask your own question. Some starting points from the manual: Do preferred substances share an ingredient? Does a phototactic response override a chemotactic one? Does fly age change geotaxis? Do mutant flies choose differently? Does the flies' choice depend on smell or on sight (try Parafilm)?
