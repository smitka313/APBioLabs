Enzyme Activity

How do abiotic or biotic factors influence the rates of enzymatic reactions?

Unit 3: Cellular EnergeticsENE-1.FENE-1.GSYI-3.AScience Practices 6.C, 6.E≈ 3–4 class periods
    Overview

    Watching an enzyme work, one shade of brown at a time

    Turnip roots are full of peroxidase, an enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of aerobic metabolism. You will mix a turnip extract with dilute hydrogen peroxide and an indicator, guaiacol, that turns brown as the reaction proceeds. The faster the browning, the faster the enzyme is working.

    What you will do
    • Procedure 1: Develop a reliable assay and establish a baseline: an enzyme tube compared with a no-enzyme control, read every minute for 5 minutes with a 1–10 color palette or a spectrophotometer at 470 nm.
    • Procedure 2: Replace the water in the enzyme tube with buffers from pH 3 to pH 10 and find the pH optimum.
    • Your investigation: Test temperature, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, heat treatment, or an inhibitor, and compare initial reaction rates.
    Learning objectives
    • ENE-1.F Explain how changes to the structure of an enzyme may affect its function.
    • ENE-1.G Explain how the cellular environment affects enzyme activity.
    • SYI-3.A Connect variation in molecules (such as different forms of an enzyme) to an organism’s ability to survive in different environments.
    • Science practices 6.C, 6.E Justify claims with evidence; predict the effects of a change in a biological system.
    Safety
    • Wear goggles, a lab coat, closed-toe shoes, and gloves. Guaiacol can irritate skin at high concentration.
    • Use pipette pumps or syringes. Never pipette by mouth. Use a separate, labeled pipette for each solution.
    • The 0.1% hydrogen peroxide and dilute guaiacol used here can go down the drain. Broken glass goes in the glass container.
    • Inhibitors such as hydroxylamine also act on human enzymes. In this lab they appear only in the simulation; do not handle them at the bench.
    Background

    Enzymes, shape, and rate

    Enzymes are protein catalysts. They speed reactions by lowering the activation energy, the energy needed to reach the unstable transition state between reactants and products. The substrate binds in the enzyme’s active site, a pocket whose shape comes from the protein’s folding: primary structure (amino acid sequence) determines the hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges that build secondary, tertiary, and sometimes quaternary structure. Anything that disturbs those interactions can change the active site and the rate.

    2 H2O2  —peroxidase→  2 H2O + O2

    The oxygen released reacts with colorless guaiacol to form tetraguaiacol, which is brown. More product means a darker tube. You can rank the color against a 1–10 palette (a relative rate) or measure absorbance of 470 nm light with a spectrophotometer (an absolute rate, such as 0.083 absorbance units per minute). By the Beer–Lambert law, absorbance is proportional to the concentration of the brown product: A = εlc.

    initial rate v = Vmax[S] ÷ (Km + [S])   ·   Vmax ∝ [enzyme]

    Temperature speeds molecular collisions, so rate rises at first (roughly doubling for each 10 °C), but heat also shakes apart the weak bonds that hold the enzyme’s shape. Above the optimum the protein unfolds; at high temperature the unfolding becomes permanent (denaturation), and cooling does not bring the activity back. pH changes the charges on amino acid side chains in and around the active site, so each enzyme works best in a narrow pH range that often matches where it normally functions. Peroxidases from different sources have optimum pH values from 4 to 11 and optimum temperatures from 10 to 70 °C. Inhibitors bind the enzyme and reduce its activity.

    A baseline is the standard reaction you compare everything else to. Because product builds up and conditions change as the reaction runs, compare treatments using the initial rate: the slope of absorbance (or color) versus time over the first minutes, when the graph is still nearly straight.

    Procedure 1 · Structured inquiry

    Developing the assay and determining a baseline

    Tube recipes (each 7.5 mL; combined 15.0 mL)
    Substrate tube: 7.0 mL distilled water + 0.3 mL 0.1% H2O2 + 0.2 mL guaiacol
    Enzyme tube: 6.0 mL distilled water + 1.5 mL turnip peroxidase
    No-enzyme control: enzyme tube with 7.5 mL water and no peroxidase
    Spectrophotometer blank: 13.3 mL water + 0.2 mL guaiacol + 1.5 mL enzyme (everything except substrate), λ = 470 nm
    Steps
    1. Combine the substrate and enzyme tubes, cover with Parafilm, invert twice, and start the timer at once.
    2. Rotate the tube before each reading and record color (or absorbance) at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 minutes. A phone photo helps.
    3. Match each photo to the palette and record a number from 1 to 10.
    4. Run at least 3 trials.
