2 · First ideas

What do you notice about peat?

Background

  • Peat is layers of dead plants that never fully rotted, found in wet places such as bogs.
  • Zombie fires smolder in peat for months, then break out again.
  • A fuel is a material that burns and gives off energy.

What this simulation models
  • The handfuls are not equal, because the Teacher Edition notes the fuels are not uniform and the demonstration did not stress equal masses. Starting masses: about 4 g (grass), 4 g (leaves), 10 g (wood chips), 10 g (charcoal) and 30 g (peat). They catch fire after about 4, 6, 14, 28 and 45 s, and burn for about 20, 30, 90, 150 and 260 s. Peat catches slowly, then smolders with smoke and a glowing ember.
  • Mass lost: about 93% (grass), 90% (leaves), 97% (wood), 88% (charcoal), 78% (peat, which keeps more ash). So peat loses the most grams, about 23 g. The mass that disappears leaves as gases, mostly carbon dioxide and water vapor. The balance reads to 0.1 g, with a little error.
  • Heat per gram burned is about 15, 16, 18, 29 and 20 kJ. The laser thermometer is used only at the two weighings, not while it burns, and the illustration aims the laser at the fuel before the burn and at the ash after the dome is lifted. The visible laser is an aiming guide. The model supplies a simulated fuel temperature; a real infrared thermometer cannot reliably measure through the glass dome. The rise is the difference between the two readings, grams burned × heat per gram × 0.35 ÷ 8, about 2 to 22 °C. It is a modeled surface temperature change, not a direct measure of heat energy: most for peat (about 20 °C), then charcoal, wood, and a little for grass and leaves. It reads to 1 °C, with a little error. Charcoal gives the most heat per gram. Real infrared readings depend on the surface, the distance and what lies between the instrument and the surface.
  • Carbon dioxide made is the grams lost times the carbon in the fuel (charcoal has the most per gram). It collects under the dome, and a fixed share dissolves into the BTB in the dish. The dome is not perfectly sealed, so some gas escapes (the Teacher Edition says the system was not totally closed). So the BTB shows a color change, not an exact amount: more mass lost and more carbon mean a bigger change, from blue toward green or yellow.
  • Hydrogen in the fuel joins oxygen and leaves as water vapor, which condenses as droplets on the inside of the dome glass: most for peat, least for charcoal.
  • Your samples differ by about 8% in mass lost and heat, and about 15% in time to catch (shown to the nearest second, so a fuel that catches after about 4 s can show 5 s), like real samples. One simulated second is one sixth of a real second. Real results also depend on moisture, sample shape, air flow and the dome.
