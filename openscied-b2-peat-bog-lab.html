1 · Overview

Plants pull carbon out of the air. What puts it back?

Welcome back. Resume

In an Arctic peat bog, plants pull carbon out of the air and store it in their bodies. When they die, the cold, soggy ground keeps them from rotting, so they pile up as peat over thousands of years. Change the conditions and see whether the bog stores carbon or releases it.

The same substances appear in every row, but the arrow points the opposite way. The outputs of photosynthesis are the inputs of respiration, and the outputs of respiration are the inputs of photosynthesis. Photosynthesis stores the sun’s energy in glucose. Respiration and burning release that energy and return the carbon to the air. Peat builds up only when photosynthesis runs faster than its opposites.

How to use this simulator

Set an exact number

Type the number in the box next to a slider and press Enter. Or click the slider’s round handle and use the left and right arrow keys.

Start over

Choose Reset under Quick scenarios, or the Reset button next to Record this trial, to put every bog setting back to the start. Reset to today resets the global warming sliders.

Read the bog picture

Moving shapes show materials moving. Thicker, faster arrows mean a faster rate. Each material has its own color and shape: gray circles = carbon dioxide, blue diamonds = oxygen, teal drops = water, purple squares = dead plants becoming peat, yellow stars = sunlight. The dashed teal line is the water level. The striped blue layer is permafrost (frozen peat). The Pause motion button stops the movement.

Read the number boxes

Rates go from 0 (stopped) to 100 (fastest). The ↑ and ↓ badges show how much a number changed. Peat carbon: positive (✓ green) means a carbon sink and the peat grows. Negative (✕ red) means carbon is released to the atmosphere and the peat shrinks.

Get more help

Choose an i button to explain a term. Choose a speaker button to hear a note read aloud with your device’s built-in voice. Nothing is sent anywhere.

Keep your data

Choose Record this trial to save the current numbers. Open My trials to see them, label them, and download a copy. Your settings and trials are saved only in this browser.

    4 · Real-world connection · Arctic wildfires

    Zombie fires

    Some Arctic peat fires never really go out. After the flames die in the fall, the fire keeps smoldering underground all winter, hidden under the snow. In spring it bursts back to the surface. Scientists call these overwintering or “zombie” fires. They have come back in Alaska, Canada, and Siberia in recent years, as warmer summers dry out and thaw more peat.

    5 · Bonus · Earth science connection

    Carbon dioxide and global warming

    Photosynthesis pulls CO₂ out of the air. Decomposition and burning put it back. Peat, coal, oil, and gas all formed from ancient plants, so burning them releases carbon that photosynthesis stored long ago. When more CO₂ goes into the air than plants and oceans can remove, it builds up and traps heat.

    Why CO₂ traps heat

    Sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground. The ground gives off heat as infrared energy. CO₂ molecules absorb that heat and send some of it back down, like a blanket.

    Why it matters for peat

    Warming thaws permafrost and dries out bogs. Decomposers wake up, and dry peat can burn, even as zombie fires that last through the winter. Both release more CO₂, which causes more warming. Scientists call this a feedback loop.

    This is a simplified model for learning. Rates are on a 0–100 scale chosen to show trends, not real measurements. Real climate projections include many more factors, such as other greenhouse gases, clouds, ice, and how oceans store heat.

    Investigations to try
    • High tiltSet the tilt to 24.2°, like about 10,700 years ago. What happens to summer sunlight, temperature, and photosynthesis in the Arctic?
    • Low tiltSet the tilt to 22.1°. How much of the peat freezes into permafrost? Why can’t decomposers or fire reach frozen peat?
    • Why peat builds upLower the water level from 80% to 10%. What happens to the oxygen in the peat, and to decomposition?
    • Peat fireSet burning to 60%. Try it with a wet bog, then a dry bog. Which one releases more CO₂?
    • Opposites cancel outFind settings where photosynthesis and decomposition are equal and there is no fire. What happens to the peat? Why does carbon stop building up?
    • Compare the outputsDecomposition and burning both release CO₂. What inputs do they share? How is the energy released differently?
    • Zombie fireStart a fire, then raise the water level a little at a time. At what water level does the fire stop surviving the winter? Why does water put it out?
    • ThawChoose “Arctic heat wave.” Is the bog still a carbon sink, or is carbon being released to the atmosphere?

    7 · About this model

    What this simulation models

    Simulated. Everything on this page comes from a teaching model. It shows trends. It does not predict any real bog.

    Also in Unit B.2: Fires: Peat, Decomposition and Earth’s Tilt (Lessons 3 and 5).

    What is modeled

    • Summer sunlight at 65°N from Earth’s tilt, using the standard solstice formula.
    • Photosynthesis, decomposition and burning as rates from 0 to 100, set by sunlight, temperature, CO₂, oxygen and water.
    • Frozen peat (permafrost) that decomposers and fire cannot reach.
    • Oxygen reaching the peat, which falls as the water level rises.
    • The chance a smoldering fire lasts through the winter.
    • A carbon budget to 2100: fossil fuels and land clearing add carbon, plants and oceans remove it, and CO₂ drives warming.

    What is simplified

    • The 0–100 scale is a teaching scale. It is not grams of carbon or any real unit.
    • Peat is shown as “glucose.” Real peat is layers of partly decomposed plants (cellulose, lignin and more).
    • The whole bog has one water level and one oxygen number. Real peat has oxygen near the surface and almost none below the water.
    • Sunlight uses Earth’s average distance from the sun and ignores clouds.
    • Tilt warms the summer by a simple rule (0.1 °C for each extra W/m²).
    • The warming model uses 2.0 °C for each doubling of CO₂ and leaves out other gases, clouds, ice and the ocean’s slow heat storage.
    • Nothing is random, so everyone gets the same numbers for the same settings.

    How real bogs differ

    • Without oxygen, decomposition slows a lot but does not stop. Some microbes keep working and make methane, another greenhouse gas.
    • A flaming fire needs about 15–16% oxygen. A smoldering peat fire can keep going on less. The model uses 12% as one middle value.
    • Real fires depend on peat depth, snow cover, wind, lightning and people, not only on the five bars shown here.
    • Real bogs differ a lot from place to place, and so do the numbers.
    • Estimates of how much faster the Arctic warms run from about 2 to 4 times the planet’s average. The model uses 2.5.
    The rules the model uses
    • Photosynthesis = 100 × light × CO₂ × temperature × water × oxygen. Each factor is a number from about 0 to 1.2, and the result is held between 0 and 100. More light and more CO₂ raise it. It peaks near 18 °C. Very dry or very hot bogs slow it.
    • Decomposition doubles for each 10 °C of warming, rises with oxygen in the peat, and falls toward 0 as the peat freezes.
    • Burning rises with the Burning slider and with dry peat and oxygen, and falls to 0 at or below 12% oxygen or in frozen peat.
    • Peat carbon = photosynthesis − decomposition − burning. Above 0 is a carbon sink. Below 0 is a carbon source.
