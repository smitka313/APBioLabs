EXHIBIT E

Who was diagnosed at an early stage?

Researchers used the California Cancer Registry to compare stage at diagnosis for 1,706,573 people age 15 or older with one of nine cancers, 2006–2020.

What do these groups and percentages mean?

Early stage here means in situ or localized: the cancer had not spread beyond where it started. Persistent poverty means a neighborhood poverty rate of at least 20% for roughly 30 years. It does not tell us a patient’s income.

Persistent-poverty neighborhoods: 47,426 of 101,820 diagnosed patients. Other neighborhoods: 933,285 of 1,604,753. These totals include people whose stage was unknown (8.3% and 5.7%, respectively). The chart does not show what caused the difference.

California Cancer Registry, 2006–2020. Original study, Table 1.

EXHIBIT F

What happened when patients faced higher costs?

From 2004–2014, some U.S. employers switched workers to health plans with annual deductibles of $1,000 or more. The study compared women who switched with similar women who kept plans with deductibles of $500 or less.

What did the study measure?

A deductible is the amount a patient pays for certain covered services before insurance begins to pay. The dollar amounts describe the plan’s annual deductible, not the price of each test. Here, “higher-poverty” means at least 20% of households lived below the federal poverty line. It does not tell us each patient’s income.

The study estimated differences in when groups reached the same benchmark for each step. The bars are separate comparisons, not one patient’s wait times or steps to add together. Employers made the switch, but the study was not randomized. It does not show whether cancers had spread farther or whether a less expensive test works as well.

What did patients actually pay? See example costs

A separate analysis of commercial insurance claims from January–June 2023 reported these average amounts paid by patients for follow-up breast tests:

  • Ultrasound: $108.15
  • Diagnostic mammogram: $144.84
  • MRI: $256.61
  • Biopsy: $446.93

These are averages from a different group, not prices for the people in the chart. These tests do different jobs. A lower price doesn’t mean one can replace another.

American Cancer Society Cancer Action Network, Figure 2.

01 · Initial hunch

Will the rankings match?

Real U.S. data

Imagine two lists of cancers: one ranked by new diagnoses, and one by deaths. Will the same cancer be at the top of both lists?

Two different measurements

Incidence means new diagnoses; mortality means deaths. A rate per 100,000 puts each group on the same scale so you can compare groups of different sizes. For example, 20 diagnoses among 200,000 people in one year is 10 diagnoses per 100,000 people that year.

What does age-adjusted mean?

Age-adjusted rates also account for groups having different mixes of younger and older people by using the same standard age mix.

We use group statistics. No one needs to share personal or family health information.

Your investigation question:

A rate per 100,000 puts populations of different sizes on the same scale. It is not a raw count.

Does access change what gets detected?

Predict whether urban and higher-income communities will show more screening, more recorded diagnoses, both, or neither. Then compare counties and look for a counterexample. A diagnosis tells you a cancer was found and recorded, not when it began.

Check your hypothesis against national evidence

In a 2022 CDC survey, 77.2% of women ages 50–74 in metro areas reported a mammogram in the previous two years, compared with 73.1% outside metros. The estimates were 82.6% for households earning $75,000 or more and 63.1% below $15,000.

In CDC registry data for 2018–2022, female breast diagnoses per 100,000 were higher in metro counties. Lung and colorectal diagnoses showed the opposite pattern. These are different datasets, not the same people or proof of why the rates differ.

Investigate access: screening → follow-up testing → training
  1. FDA mammography facility finder: Can you find a screening site near each county you picked?
  2. ACR imaging facility finder: Search Michigan and look for breast ultrasound or other accredited imaging. Which follow-up services are listed? Check for confirmed accreditation, since this directory also includes applications in progress. An empty search is not proof a service is unavailable.
  3. CAAHEP program finder: Search Michigan for Diagnostic Medical Sonography. A program listing shows a training location, not the number of places available or how often working staff can practice.

Record what you find and what you still need to know. Distance, referral, appointment availability, cost, staff, equipment and supplies can all matter. Breast ultrasound can be used for follow-up or as a supplement, not as a replacement for routine mammography.

        Data sources and what the charts can’t tell us

        These data describe groups, not individual risk or genetics. A graph can suggest a question, but it does not prove what caused a difference. Age adjustment makes some comparisons fairer; it does not control for things such as screening, environmental exposure, or access to care.

        Counts and rates answer different questions. The people diagnosed in a year are not necessarily the same people who died that year. Dividing one published rate by the other does not tell you the chance of surviving.
