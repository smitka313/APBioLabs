01 · Your starting idea

Will the rankings match?

Real U.S. data

Imagine two lists of cancers: one ranked by new diagnoses, and one by deaths. Will the same cancer be at the top of both lists?

Two different measurements

Incidence counts new diagnoses. Mortality counts deaths. Each is shown as a rate per 100,000 in the population named by that source.

Why use a rate?

Rates make different-sized populations easier to compare. For example, 20 events out of 200,000 people equals 10 per 100,000. Age-adjusted rates take it a step further by using the same standard age mix.

We use group statistics. No one needs to share personal or family health information.

    Data sources and what the charts can’t tell us

    These data describe groups, not individual risk or genetics. A graph can suggest a question, but it does not prove what caused a difference. Age adjustment makes some comparisons fairer; it does not control for things such as screening, environmental exposure, or access to care.

    Counts and rates answer different questions. The people diagnosed in a year are not necessarily the same people who died that year. Dividing one published rate by the other does not tell you the chance of surviving.
