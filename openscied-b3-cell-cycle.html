1 · Overview

Your investigation

Cells copy their DNA and divide as organisms grow and repair tissues. Cell-cycle controls can pause division when a cell needs more resources or when its DNA needs attention. In this activity, you will model those controls, then test how the number of cell divisions changes across different conditions.

Ask your teacher which route to follow. The full investigation takes two periods: play the game first, then run and analyze the growth model.

Short route · one class period
  1. Open the growth model. Write your prediction. Run skin at age 50 with average body size, then change only age to 70 and run again.
  2. Analyze: complete Q1, Q2, Q4a, and one limitation. You can skip the game and the other questions for this route.
  3. Wrap up: preview and save your report. It can say “In progress” for the full activity; it is still the work to submit for this shorter route.

You will use two connected models:

  1. Play the Cell Game. In Round 1, p53 checks your cell at every checkpoint. In Round 2, Cellular Chance cards sometimes stop p53 from working. Count the blue (non-cancer) and red (cancer) cell chips your cells make, then make sense of what happened.
  2. Run the growth model. Compare age, body size, and cell-division frequency. Collect repeated results and look for a pattern.
By the end, you should be able to
  • Describe how cell-cycle checkpoints can pause or stop cell division.
  • Explain how p53 can help cells respond to DNA damage.
  • Use model data to connect more cell divisions with more opportunities for DNA-copying changes.
  • Identify at least one thing these models leave out.
Working on your own?

Everything you need is on this page. Directions are broken into short steps, and the activity gives hints after each prediction. You do not need a partner or class data.

A note about cancer

This model shows one part of a complex process. It does not predict anyone’s personal cancer risk. Cancer is not inevitable, and one DNA change alone does not mean a person will develop cancer.

2 · Get ready

Four parts of a cell cycle

G1GrowGather materials
SCopy DNAMake a DNA copy
G2CheckPrepare to divide
MDivideOne cell becomes two

Cell-cycle controls respond to a cell’s condition. If something is wrong, the cycle may pause so the cell can respond. The p53 protein helps cells respond to some kinds of stress and DNA damage. If damage is too severe, a cell may stop dividing or die.

Quick vocabulary help

Checkpoint: a control point where the cell cycle can pause before moving on.

DNA-copying change: a change in DNA sequence. A change can have different effects; it does not automatically cause cancer.

Proto-oncogene: a gene that helps cells grow and divide when signaled.

Oncogene: a changed proto-oncogene that can keep sending a “divide” signal.

Tumor-suppressor gene: a gene, such as TP53 (which codes for p53), that slows or stops division or helps repair DNA. A change can stop it from working, like losing a brake.

Model: a simplified representation used to explore an idea. A model helps with some questions and leaves others unanswered.

Quick check: What is the most accurate idea?

3 · The cell cycle, in play

A cell’s journey.

This is the OpenSciEd Cell Game, played on screen with the same board, cards and scorecard as your handout. Move your cell one space each turn and roll four dice to check off nutrients, proteins, organelles and chromosomes. At each checkpoint, p53 checks your scorecard.

What this game models

The Cell Game is a game model of how a cell grows and divides. In Round 1, p53 works at every checkpoint. In Round 2, Cellular Chance cards can stop p53 from working, and some cells become cancer cells (red chips) in this game model. The game cannot predict anyone’s health: cancer is not inevitable, and no game result is a diagnosis.

In real cells: More than one protein controls the cycle. The G1/S checkpoint can pause before DNA copying. The G2/M checkpoint can delay division if copying is incomplete or DNA is damaged, giving the cell a chance to repair. The spindle checkpoint makes sure chromosomes are attached to the fibers that separate them. A pause in a real cell is not a move back on the board.

Set up the board

Take a cell through its cycle.

Each player starts with one non-cancer cell (a blue chip) on START. Each round gives every player up to 75 turns; every time your cell divides you collect a new chip and start a new cell (a division does not end the round). The round ends when every player has used 75 turns, or sooner if your group chooses End this round now. Play on your own or share a device with up to four players.

The Cell Game now follows the OpenSciEd board and cards exactly. Rounds you finished with the earlier version are still listed in your evidence; play the rounds again to count your cell chips.

Players
Take turns in player-number order, like going clockwise around the table. There is no winner.

What each phase needs before the cell moves on

  1. G1Grow & gather

    6 spaces. Check off a nutrient for each 1, a protein for each 2 and an organelle for each 3 you roll.

    G1 Checkpoint: at least 3 nutrients, 3 proteins and 3 organelles to pass. A 2 in any category: go back 3 spaces. A 1: go back 5 spaces. A 0: destroyed by p53, back to the START.

    Why: p53 checks that the cell has grown and gathered enough before it makes new chromosomes. A cell that is short is sent back to keep gathering, or destroyed.

  2. SChromosomes

    8 spaces. Check off a chromosome for each 4 you roll (4 boxes).

    Goal: 4 DNA (all 4 Chromosomes boxes). The G2 Checkpoint checks this.

    Why: the cell needs all of its chromosomes before it can divide into two cells.

  3. G2Grow & gather

    8 spaces. Check off nutrients, proteins and organelles again (1, 2 and 3).

    G2 Checkpoint: at least 4 DNA and at least 1 nutrient, 1 protein and 1 organelle to pass. Fewer than 4 DNA: go back to the first S space. A 0 in any category: destroyed by p53, back to the START.

    Why: p53 checks the chromosomes and materials once more, so a cell does not start dividing without them.

  4. MDivide

    5 spaces marked Must roll 1 to 5. To leave the G2 Checkpoint you must roll a 1; to move to each next space you must roll its number. If you do not, you do not move.

    M Checkpoint: double-check the whole scorecard (G1: 3 of each; S: 4 DNA; G2: 1 of each). Complete: YOU SUCCESSFULLY DIVIDED! Take a new blue cell chip.

    Why: this is the last check before one cell becomes two. When p53 is working, only cells with everything they need divide.

How to play

Your turn: On your first turn, move your piece to the first G1 space and roll all four dice. The numbers you roll do not tell you how many spaces to move: move forward only one space on every turn unless a card tells you otherwise.

Scorecard: In Phase G1 and Phase G2, check off a nutrient for each 1, a protein for each 2 and an organelle for each 3 (5 boxes each). In Phase S, check off a chromosome for each 4 (4 boxes). Ignore all other numbers.

Checkpoints and p53: When you reach a checkpoint, turn over its card (the game does this for you): p53 checks your scorecard. G1 Checkpoint: pass with at least 3 nutrients, 3 proteins and 3 organelles; a 2 in any category sends you back 3 spaces, a 1 sends you back 5 spaces, and a 0 means YOU HAVE BEEN DESTROYED by p53 and go back to the START. G2 Checkpoint: pass with at least 4 DNA and 1 nutrient, 1 protein and 1 organelle; fewer than 4 DNA sends you back to the first S space, and a 0 in any category means destroyed by p53, back to the START. A cell sent back keeps its check marks; a destroyed cell starts again with a new scorecard.

Phase M: To proceed past the G2 Checkpoint, you must roll a 1. To proceed to each next space you must roll the number on that space (2, then 3, 4 and 5). You may not skip any spaces; if you do not roll the number you need, you do not move during that turn.

M Checkpoint and FINISH: p53 double-checks the whole scorecard. If it is complete, YOU SUCCESSFULLY DIVIDED: take a new blue cell chip (a non-cancer cell). Your next turn starts a new cell on the first G1 space with a new scorecard. Dividing does not end the round: keep playing until every player has used 75 turns (the turn limit), or end the round sooner with End this round now (for example, when your class timer runs out). If the double-check finds anything missing (possible only after a Round 2 card skipped a checkpoint), p53 destroys the cell and it goes back to the START.

Round 2: The directions are the same except every time you land on a light-blue square, you draw a Cellular Chance card and follow the directions on it; the card then goes back on the bottom of the stack (13 cards). The light-blue squares are G1 spaces 2, 4 and 6; S spaces 1, 3, 5 and 7; G2 spaces 1, 3, 5 and 7; and M spaces 2 and 4. On the board they are filled light blue. Cards are drawn when your one-space move lands on a light-blue square, not when a card or checkpoint moves your piece there.

Red chips: “p53 is not working!” cards let a cell skip checkpoints. A cell sent directly to FINISH by a card collects a RED cell chip, a cancer cell in this game model. A cell that skips the M Checkpoint and reaches FINISH also collects a red chip if its scorecard is missing anything (a blue chip if it is complete).

    What the model includes—and leaves out

    These are invented teaching units, not human cell counts or cancer probabilities. Each modeled cell unit has one chance to divide per modeled year. These invented chances are 18% for renewing skin cells, 60% for colon-lining renewal cells, 2.5% for brain glial/support cells, 55% for red-cell precursors, and 45% for white-cell precursors. These values help compare patterns; they are not real turnover measurements. The model keeps the number of cell units constant and counts division events from age 20 onward. The body-size setting stands for height: a taller person has more cells of each type, modeled here as 20, 30 or 40 cell units. More cells means more chances for divisions and changes, not that any tall person will get cancer; this is a model, not a prediction for any individual.

    Each division creates an opportunity to copy DNA. A division does not guarantee a mutation or cancer. On each division, the model has a 3% chance to alter one of three controls (still far higher than in real cells): growth signaling (a proto-oncogene becoming an oncogene), checkpoint braking (a tumor-suppressor gene such as p53 losing function), or DNA repair (a repair gene losing function). In this model, a control change is a mutation: a change in the DNA of one cell unit that alters one of the three controls. A cell enters the magenta cancerous state only after all three modeled controls are altered; it then has an 80% chance to divide per modeled year. A single change does not turn it cancerous. Counts need not rise exactly in proportion to elapsed years: random variation affects every run, and units that enter the cancerous state use the higher 80% annual division chance in subsequent years. Inspect a unit to see its recorded divisions and its division chance for the next modeled year. This three-control rule is a teaching simplification, not a real diagnostic criterion or a claim that three changes always cause cancer. Real cells also repair most DNA damage, can self-destruct through apoptosis when damage is severe, and can be cleared by the immune system; the model leaves out these protections, along with many genes, exposures, and differences among people. Because of this, most trials end with no cancer-state units, and cancer is not inevitable in the model or in people. In real bone marrow, the long-lived blood-forming stem cells divide rarely and are protected in ways this model leaves out, while most quickly dividing precursors mature and leave the marrow; do not read the blood settings as a ranking of real cancer risk. Playback compresses years into seconds. The highlighted body region follows the chosen tissue. All drawings are schematic. Blood samples represent bone-marrow precursors; the brain sample represents a glial/support-cell population, not mature neurons. The cell count stays fixed even after cells enter the cancerous state; this model does not simulate tumor size or spread.
