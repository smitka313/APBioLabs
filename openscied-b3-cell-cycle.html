1 · Overview

Your investigation

Cells copy their DNA and divide as organisms grow and repair tissues. Cell-cycle controls can pause division when a cell needs more resources or when its DNA needs attention. In this activity, you will model those controls, then test how the number of cell divisions changes across different conditions.

You will use two connected models:

  1. Play the cell-cycle game. Make predictions about what happens at checkpoints when controls are working and when one control is disrupted.
  2. Run the growth model. Compare age, body size, and cell-division frequency. Collect repeated results and look for a pattern.
By the end, you should be able to
  • Describe how cell-cycle checkpoints can pause or stop cell division.
  • Explain how p53 can help cells respond to DNA damage.
  • Use model data to connect more cell divisions with more opportunities for DNA-copying changes.
  • Identify at least one thing these models leave out.
Working on your own?

Everything you need is on this page. Directions are broken into short steps, and the activity gives hints after each prediction. You do not need a partner or class data.

A note about cancer

This model shows one part of a complex process. It does not predict anyone’s personal cancer risk. Cancer is not inevitable, and one DNA change alone does not mean a person will develop cancer.

Cell-cycle controls respond to a cell’s condition. If something is wrong, the cycle may pause so the cell can respond. The p53 protein helps cells respond to some kinds of stress and DNA damage. If damage is too severe, a cell may stop dividing or die.

Quick vocabulary help

Checkpoint: a control point where the cell cycle can pause before moving on.

DNA-copying change: a change in DNA sequence. A change can have different effects; it does not automatically cause cancer.

Model: a simplified representation used to explore an idea. A model helps with some questions and leaves others unanswered.

3 · Cell-cycle game

Will this cell move forward?

You will see four cell conditions. Predict what the cell-cycle controls do, then reveal the model outcome. In Group mode, players share one device and take turns. The group is investigating together—there is no winner or score.

Choose how to play
Use Player 1, Player 2, and so on. You do not need to enter names.

Group turns rotate through the four conditions. Take a turn, discuss your reasoning, and pass the device. Your predictions stay on this device.

How to read the rounds

Round 1 represents functioning cell-cycle controls. Round 2 represents a disrupted checkpoint response. This is a simplified model of one pathway, not a complete explanation of how cancer develops.

4 · Growth model

How many chances are there for a DNA-copying change?

In this model, different cell types divide at different rates. A larger modeled body has more cells in the system, and a longer time gives those cells more opportunities to divide. Each division is one more opportunity for a DNA-copying change—not a guarantee that a change happens.

Model limits: The numbers are made-up model units, not real human cell counts, mutation rates, or cancer probabilities. Use them to compare patterns, not to predict a person’s health.
The model follows cell activity from age 20 to your selected age.
Cell units are simplified; they are not literal cell counts.

Your results

Compare one factor at a time
  1. Run three trials with the settings you have now.
  2. Change only age, body size, or cell type.
  3. Run three more trials and compare the results.

Changing one setting at a time makes it easier to connect the evidence to that factor.
