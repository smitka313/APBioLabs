Two lessons, one investigation

Welcome back. Resume

In the Cell Game, cells that finished mitosis with matching chromosomes were non-cancer cells, and cells whose chromosomes did not match were cancer cells. This activity asks what could make chromosomes different, and what p53 has to do with it.

By the end, you should be able to
  • Describe how DNA replication copies a strand, one matching letter at a time.
  • Explain how a copying mistake (a substitution) becomes a mutation that can be passed on.
  • Explain what p53 does in the model, and why only one of the three versions works.
  • Describe how a gene’s DNA code becomes a protein, and how a small DNA change can change a protein’s shape.

Quick vocabulary help

DNA: a very long molecule that stores a cell’s instructions in the order of four letters: A, T, G and C.

Chromosome: one very long piece of DNA, wrapped up tightly when a cell is about to divide.

Replication: making a copy of DNA before a cell divides. It happens in S phase.

Mutation: a change in the DNA sequence. A substitution is one letter swapped for another.

p53: a protein that helps a cell respond to DNA damage.

Part A · Lesson 5 · 3 · Computer model

The DNA fork

You will copy a short piece of DNA, 24 letters long, the way a cell does it in S phase. Work through these steps in the model:

  1. Unwind. The wound-up DNA has to loosen so the letters can be reached.
  2. Unzip. A molecule called helicase pulls the two strands apart, like a zipper.
  3. Pair the letters. Match each exposed letter with its partner: A with T and G with C. You do five pairs on each strand, then the model speeds up and finishes the rest.
  4. Count the substitutions. Some automatic pairings are wrong. A wrong letter is a substitution. Correct pairs show a green letter and substitutions show a red letter with a ✕ mark, so you never need color alone.
  5. Try p53. Test each of the three versions, p53 (a), p53 (b) and p53 (c), on both strands. Replace each letter that p53 removes with the correct one until both strands have zero substitutions.

What this simulation models

Modeled: unwinding, unzipping, pairing A with T and G with C, occasional wrong pairings (substitutions), and three versions of p53.

Simplified: a real cell copies billions of letters, not 24. Real replication uses many other proteins, and the cell’s copying enzymes also proofread as they go. This model leaves them out so you can see the main idea. Only substitutions are modeled; real mutations also include letters added or left out.

About p53: in the model, p53 attaches to the finished strand and removes a wrong letter. In a real cell, p53 works more like a manager. It senses DNA damage and switches on genes that pause the cell cycle, call in repair proteins, or, if the damage is too great, tell the cell to die. Other proteins do most of the actual repair.

Why the versions differ: in the model only p53 (a) fits the strand and works. Real people can carry changed forms of p53 that work worse or not at all.

Short reading

DNA and chromosomes

Almost every cell in your body has a nucleus, and the nucleus holds your DNA. DNA is a very long molecule built from small units called nucleotides. Each unit has a sugar, a phosphate and one of four bases: A, T, G or C. The order of the bases is the genetic code.

The bases pair up in a fixed way. A goes with T, and G goes with C. These are complementary base pairs, and they hold the two strands of the double helix together.

Most of the time DNA is loose, like a tangled ball of yarn. Before a cell divides, each DNA molecule is packed tightly into a chromosome. One chromosome is one very long DNA molecule. A gene is a section of a chromosome with the instructions for one protein, and people have about 20,000 genes that code for proteins.

Before mitosis, in S phase, the cell copies all of its DNA. This is DNA replication. The DNA unwinds and unzips, and each exposed letter picks up its partner. A human cell copies billions of letters this way (about 6 billion in all, counting the set from each parent).

Replication is remarkably accurate, but not perfect. Sometimes a wrong letter goes in. This kind of mistake is a substitution, and a change in the DNA is called a mutation. If a mutation stays, it is copied into the new cells, and their chromosomes are no longer identical to the parent’s. Most mutations are harmless, and cells have ways to fix many of them. Cancer usually takes several changes in genes that control cell division.

Check my checklist against the key ideas

Compare after you have filled in your own. Your wording will be different, and that is fine. Look for any idea you left out.

  • The relationship between DNA and chromosomes. Chromosomes are made of DNA (reading).
  • Why, how and when DNA replicates. DNA is copied during S phase, so each new cell can have a full set (Cell Game, Lesson 3).
  • The process of replication. The DNA unwinds and unzips, then matching letters pair up: A with T, G with C (simulation).
  • Where, how and why errors occur during replication. A wrong letter is sometimes paired during copying. That is a substitution, a kind of mutation (simulation).
  • Where, how and why errors are corrected. In the model, p53 finds a mismatch in the finished copy and removes it so the right letter can go in. Only one of the three versions works (simulation).
  • What happens to the DNA after replication. Each new cell gets a copy (Cell Game, Lesson 3).
  • How DNA with mutations relates to cancer cells with differences in their chromosomes. A mutation that is not fixed is copied into the new cell, so its chromosomes differ. Some cells like this can become cancer cells, but most changes alone do not cause cancer.

Watch out for this: in the model, p53 fixes a mistake after the copy is made. It does not stop mistakes from happening.

Part B · Lesson 6 · 7 · Gene expression model

How do we make p53?

Lesson question

How do we make p53, and why is it different sometimes?

Think first: where does p53 come from? Why might there be different versions of p53? Then follow one gene from DNA to protein. You will get one of three DNA strands, A, B or C. They are almost the same.

  1. Location 1, the nucleus. Choose a DNA strand and write its copy: pair each letter with its complement. In the copy, T in the DNA pairs with A, and A in the DNA pairs with U, because RNA uses U instead of T.
  2. Edit the copy. The copy is in groups of three letters. Cross out groups 2, 3, 5 and 6, then rewrite what is left. This stands in for the parts of real RNA that are cut out before it leaves the nucleus.
  3. Location 2, the ribosome. For each group of three, find the card with the complementary code. Write the amino acid on the card, then the word under it. In order, the words make a sentence.

Text description of the model: DNA in the nucleus, an RNA copy, and a ribosome that builds a chain of amino acids.

What this simulation models

Modeled: copying DNA into RNA by complementary pairing (with U in place of T), cutting out parts of the copy, and reading the RNA in groups of three to pick amino acids that form a chain.

Simplified: the cards and the “sentence” are teaching tools. In a real cell, a protein is a long chain of amino acids that folds into a 3D shape; the shape is what lets it do its job. Real RNA editing (splicing) removes sections of different sizes, not groups 2, 3, 5 and 6, and a real cell’s copy and editing can also make mistakes.

Where the cards differ from the real genetic code: the real code table has one amino acid for each group of three. All the cards agree with it except one. The card for the RNA group CUU (card code GAA) says Pro, but in the standard code CUU codes for Leu. Part B · Lesson 6 · 8 · Making sense

Connect the model to the cell

Short reading

DNA and p53

Your DNA is inside the nucleus of each cell, organized into 23 pairs of chromosomes. Each chromosome has regions called genes. The gene for p53 is on chromosome 17. Real genes are long: the p53 gene is roughly 18,000 to 20,000 base pairs. The model strands you worked with had only 36 to 39.

Each gene has the code for one protein. Proteins do much of a cell’s work. Some build skin, hair and muscle, some speed up chemical reactions, and some, like p53, help respond to damaged DNA.

Making a protein has three steps:

  1. The code in the DNA is copied into RNA. RNA is much like DNA but has U where DNA has T.
  2. The RNA is edited. Parts that are not protein code can be removed, and sometimes different parts of a gene are used.
  3. The edited RNA leaves the nucleus and goes to a ribosome. The ribosome reads it in groups of three letters and joins amino acids into a chain. The chain folds into a protein.

Each strand you worked with, A, B or C, had a different DNA code, so each made a different amino acid chain and a protein with a different shape. Your words made sentences with different meanings in the same way. Shape matters because a protein works by how it fits with other molecules, such as DNA.

Not all DNA codes for a protein. Some stretches help turn genes on or off, some help hold chromosomes together, and some have no known job.

The lesson’s numbers, checked
  • DNA: the lesson says the p53 gene has 18,000 base pairs, and the model strands had 36–39. Real databases give roughly 18,000 to 20,000 base pairs, depending on where the gene is said to start and end. The model is about 500 times shorter.
  • Amino acids: the lesson says p53 has 375 amino acids, and the model had 8 or 9. The usual full-length human p53 protein has 393 amino acids, and shorter forms exist. The model is about 40 times shorter either way.

The point of the comparison is the same with either number: real genes and proteins are much bigger than the model.

Glossary
Amino acid
A small unit. Proteins are chains of amino acids.
Base
One of the letters in DNA: A, T, G or C (U in RNA).
Complementary
Fitting together as a pair: A with T, G with C (A with U when making RNA).
Gene
A section of DNA with the code for a protein.
Gene expression
Using a gene to make a protein.
Mutation
A change in the DNA sequence.
Protein
A molecule made of amino acids that does work in the cell. Its shape matters.
Ribosome
The cell part that reads RNA and builds proteins.
RNA
A copy of a gene’s code that travels from the nucleus.
Substitution
A mutation where one letter is swapped for another.
