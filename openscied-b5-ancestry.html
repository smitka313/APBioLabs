Sequence bench

Find what the letters can tell you

These are the first 72 bases of the real sequences from the lesson. Each column lines up the same position in two bears’ DNA. Select every column where the letters differ. A and A match; A and G do not. With a keyboard, use arrow keys to move between columns and Space to mark one.

Comparison log

This dataset compares bears, not human ancestry. A match in this short stretch does not mean two bears have identical DNA. We are looking at one piece of one gene, not whole genomes.

Explore the full eight-species dataset

Read all eight original sequences from the same noncoding region of the transthyretin gene. The full sequences differ in length; they need to be lined up with gap positions rather than compared letter-for-letter from the start.

Two lengths, two kinds of comparison. The guided worksheet counts substitutions across just the first 72 bases. Two pairs can tie there and differ once you count changes across the full pair, including gaps. The original eight-species alignment uses the pattern across all eight bears, not a single pair count.

Show how the full sequences line up

| = matching bases · × = changed base · + = gap (a base is present in only one row). Positions here number aligned columns, not the ungapped source.

Optional: align all 8 in MAFFT

The full comparison uses the fewest single-letter substitutions, insertions, or deletions to turn one sequence into the other. It is a simplified pair comparison, not the eight-species MAFFT tree used in the original lesson. The two methods can give different results: in the full raw data, this simple count ties for polar–brown and brown–American black. A tree-building analysis lines up all eight sequences and considers the pattern across species.
