Serengeti Field Notebook

Unit 1 · Lesson 2 · 25–35 min · selected stations

Investigation: Why did people protect the Serengeti, and how did they decide what to protect?

In class

  1. Write two different investigation questions.
  2. Compare two months on the map. Pin or note evidence from two stations.
  3. Use two pieces of evidence to explain a boundary decision. Write one new question.

Turn in: Preview report: turn in your explanation, station evidence, and new question.

If a resource is blocked: If a video is blocked, use the history and written station exhibits.

Migration Map Explorer

Unit 1 · Lessons 3–4 · 40–50 min · selected questions

Investigation: What might explain why wildebeest move through the Serengeti during the year?

In class

  1. Compare two months on the map. Pause and save a note about each month.
  2. In Investigate evidence, compare the same anthrax measure across June or July groups.
  3. Explain what the evidence supports. Cite the month, group, and measure.

Turn in: Turn in a draft report with the two saved observations and an evidence-based explanation.

If a resource is blocked: If the interview is blocked, use the field health cards and their matching graph.

Carrying Capacity

Unit 1 · Lesson 5 · 45–55 min · grass model

Investigation: How does grass availability affect the number of wildebeest the model can support?

In class

  1. Save a prediction and run the baseline grass model.

Serengeti Board Game

Unit 1 · Lesson 7 · 45–60 min · includes analysis

Investigation: How do migration and predator–prey encounters change the modeled herd?

In class

  1. Choose solo or numbered group roles. Review the dice example before the first hunt.
  2. Play from winter through fall. Keep track of events and changes in the record.
  3. Choose a numbered event or hunt. Use it to explain one herd change and a model limit.

Turn in: Turn in the board’s report with the event number and your analysis. Group reports need no names.

If a resource is blocked: Use the written How to play and dice example if the walkthrough video is blocked.

Burning Fuels: What Burns and Why

Unit 2 · Lesson 2 · About 40 min

Investigation: What can simulated burning tell us about why peat can fuel a fire?

In class

  1. Predict the temperature change. Weigh each fuel and take its starting temperature.
  2. Simulate all five burns. Reweigh and reread temperature to save each run.
  3. Compare mass loss, temperature change, and indicator color. Explain one fair-test limit.

Turn in: Turn in the report with recorded burns and an evidence-based explanation.

If a resource is blocked: If the simulation will not run, analyze teacher-provided readings. Do not burn fuels at home.

Investigation: What do cancer diagnosis and death rates tell us, and what do they leave out?

Cancer Screening Design Lab

Unit 3 · Engineering extension after the Data Dive · 25–30 min

Investigation: How could a clinic network improve access to needed care within its budget?

In class

  1. Read the three fictional cases and run the hospital plan. Note each person’s care needs, costs, and travel time.
  2. Move the ultrasound machine and trained operator together. Run a redesign and check the same three cases in both plans.
  3. In Recommend a plan, use patient and clinic costs as evidence. Name who could still miss care and finish.

Turn in: Submit the finished report with both plans, all three checked comparisons, and your recommendation. A winning design is not required.

If a resource is blocked: Without a device, use a teacher-provided district results table and cost sheets. Compare the same cases on paper. The fictional credits and imaging outcomes are not medical advice.

Cell Cycle & Cancer

Unit 3 · Lessons 3–4; short route uses Lesson 4 · About 40 min for the short route; 2 periods for the full activity

Investigation: How does a longer time to divide change the number of opportunities to copy DNA?

In class

  1. Skip the Cell-cycle game for this short route. In Growth model, write a prediction and run skin cells to age 50 at average body size.
  2. Run a second set to age 70 with the same tissue and body size. Both sets have three trials.
  3. In Analyze, answer Q1, Q2, and Q4a with your results. Select one model limit, then open Wrap up.

Turn in: Submit your report with both sets and the assigned answers and limit. An In progress report is expected for this short route.

If a resource is blocked: Use a teacher-provided pair of three-trial sets. Compare means and variation on paper. Simulated counts are not individual cancer risks.

DNA Workshop: Build, Copy, Decode

Unit 3 · Preparation for Lessons 5–6 · 20–30 min

Investigation: How do copying DNA, making RNA, and building an amino acid chain differ?

In class

  1. Build a nucleotide from a phosphate, a sugar, and a base. Use the pairing key to build the complementary DNA.
  2. Copy DNA and identify an original strand in each result. Make RNA using one DNA strand as the template.
  3. Decode the fictional RNA message with the codon key. Complete the final product sort.

Turn in: Show your completed final sort or submit the activity report if assigned. Written observations are optional.

If a resource is blocked: If dragging is difficult, select a piece and then its slot by touch or keyboard. Without a device, use a teacher-provided fictional sequence and printed pairing and codon keys.

DNA Copying, p53 & Gene Expression

Unit 3 · Lessons 5–6; first session uses Lesson 5 · 60–75 min total or 2 sessions by lesson

Investigation: How can a DNA copying error change the information a cell passes on?

In class

  1. Open DNA fork and record the correctly paired letters and mismatches on both strands before using the p53 model.
  2. Try version a, replace any missing letters, and record both strands again. Use the before-and-after counts to explain the result.
  3. In Making sense and Explanation, distinguish a copying error from a lasting mutation. Save your work for the next session.

Turn in: Submit your Lesson 5 report as a draft. In Lesson 6, record expression runs for strands A, B, and C and complete the remaining responses for the finished report.

If a resource is blocked: Use teacher-provided before-and-after fork evidence to write an explanation. The model simplifies DNA repair; its p53 piece does not show what the protein does directly in a cell.

Investigation: What can a controlled UV model show that a pattern across places cannot?

Hawksbeard Wind Lab

Unit 4 · Lesson 2 · 20–30 min digitally; allow extra time for the published study.

Investigation: How does the landing surface affect which hawksbeard seeds reach soil in a field and a city?

In class

  1. Predict where feathery and nonfeathery seeds will land before starting a release.
  2. Release all five matched plant pairs. Compare soil landings in the field and city.
  3. Revisit your prediction, then compare the nonfeathery share of soil landings. Name both denominators and explain a limit.

Turn in: Use Preview report to save your prediction, paired trial data, and written comparison and explanation.

If a resource is blocked: Use the physical seed-dispersal test or teacher-provided paired landing counts. On paper, compute both fractions and explain what a soil landing does not show.

Rat Generations

Unit 4 · Lesson 3 · 30–40 min for the model and explanation; reading discussion is separate.

Investigation: How can selection change which inherited alleles become more common over generations?

In class

  1. Save a prediction. Move each assigned parent pair to its nest and complete its Punnett square.
  2. Follow the colony through Gen 5. Compare births with survivors in Gen 3 and Gen 4.
  3. Use a count and a percentage to explain what changed. Keep colony size, trait percentage, and allele frequency separate.

Turn in: Save your generation record, quick check, and explanations through Preview report.

If a resource is blocked: Use the Lesson 3 bead model and paper Punnett squares. Record births and survivors, then make the same comparison from your table.

Wildlife Corridor Lab

Unit 4 · Lessons 7–9 · 25–35 min for the digital companion; keep the original reading and discussion.

Investigation: How could a road crossing change the movement of inherited alleles between separated populations?

In class

  1. Compare earlier and recent Florida panther range maps. Predict which modeled setup will make allele counts more similar.
  2. Run the connected habitat and no-crossing road for eight generations. Place a crossing and rerun from the same start.
  3. Inspect one offspring’s two inherited copies. Compare the road runs using A-copy counts and explain what the model cannot show.

Turn in: Choose Finish investigation, then save your matched runs and evidence-based explanation through Preview report.

If a resource is blocked: Provide a printed matched set of the three model runs. Have students trace one inheritance example with allele cards and compare the A-copy gaps on paper.

Investigation: Which two of the three bears share the most recent common ancestor, and what evidence supports that?

Ice Age Bear Speciation Explorer

Unit 5 · Lesson 4 · 30–40 min digitally; if split across periods, pause after the first argument.

Investigation: How might changing ice and inherited traits have contributed to the separation of bear lineages?

In class

  1. Choose a climate cycle, place bears in both phases, and check generation estimates. Save a first argument.
  2. Pin one fossil of each bear type and compare their ages and sites. Read all three gene cards and test a bear in each habitat.
  3. Revise your argument using climate, fossil, and genetic evidence. Name what remains uncertain and write one next question.

Turn in: Finish the investigation and save both arguments, fossil pins, trait matches, and conclusion check through Preview report.

If a resource is blocked: Use the Glacial and Interglacial Periods, Bear Fossils Map, and Genetic Bear Data handouts. Save the initial argument on paper before introducing the fossil and trait evidence.

Arctic Bear Futures & Hybridization

Unit 5 · Lesson 6 · 25–35 min digitally plus class discussion; use after the Lesson 5 model revision.

Investigation: What can sea-ice projections, a real bear family, and a simulation tell us about possible futures for hybrids?

In class

  1. Pin a future year. Compare lower- and higher-emissions September sea-ice area and write a prediction.
  2. Visit both islands and trace one first-generation hybrid and one backcross. Separate family evidence from encounter counts.
  3. Record ten simulated matings. Use a trait count to explain one habitat trade-off and state what the simulation cannot predict.

Turn in: Finish the investigation and save the climate comparison, family evidence, mating record, and claim through Preview report.

If a resource is blocked: Use the printed Future Predictions figure and pedigree. Use four coin flips per mating for ten matings, then tally traits and write the claim on paper.
