Unit 1 · Ecosystem Interactions & Dynamics

Start with the history of protection and the Lesson 2 field stations, trace a seasonal pattern, test a resource limit and use the board game to investigate how migration changes predator encounters.

Back to activity list

Serengeti Field Notebook

Suggested time: 25–35 minutes for selected stations, followed by group sharing. Assign before Migration Map Explorer.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Assign students two investigation questions of their own, then have partners choose different stations so they can pool their findings. Videos are optional. If a player is blocked, direct students to the history timeline, map and relevant station exhibits; each exhibit stands on its own. Have students compare a southern month with a Maasai Mara month and use the two park outlines to distinguish an observation from an explanation.
Listen for
Two relevant pieces of evidence and a distinction between the park boundary and the ecosystem. Ask who made decisions, whose perspectives were missing, and why evidence of movement mattered.
Facilitate
Use the optional organizer categories (system, components, interactions and decision-making) to support the Lesson 2 group discussion. Leave students’ questions about why animals move for Lesson 3.
Limits
The map is schematic and the evidence cards paraphrase the classroom deck. Treat examples of conservation rules as lesson context, not a guide to current land rights.

Migration Map Explorer

Suggested time: 40–50 min for selected questions; two periods for the full investigation

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Decide which months and question sets students will investigate; the activity covers material from both Lessons 3 and 4.
Facilitate
Start with a seasonal map comparison and a prediction. Select the note field or choose Pause & note to stop playback on the month students see. A typed draft appears in the report under its month, but it counts as a notebook observation only after students save it. Open Dr. Simon Mduma’s interview in Investigate if available. With or without the clip, group the health cards by month and compare the same measure across groups. Have students compare the three anthrax values in June or July. The card filter does not change the graph. The full report expects 12 month observations and 10 explanations; accept a draft for a shorter assigned set.
Listen for
Students identify the month, group and measure they are citing. Ask them to distinguish the schematic map, selected group averages for sampled male wildebeest, long-term rainfall averages and the single example year of occupancy.

Carrying Capacity

Suggested time: 45–55 min for the grass model and explanation; add 25–35 min for either extension

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Decide whether to assign the Lesson 5 grass model only, or also the separate Habitat Challenge or historical population investigation.
Facilitate
Have students predict, run the baseline, and repeat with one change in grass availability. The Round 1 recap puts the prediction beside the observed count. Compare the pattern in rounds 7–12 rather than just final counts. Students can replay without changing their saved work, or confirm replacing only the comparison. Have them revisit explanations and estimates tied to the earlier run. The full core report expects a prediction, both runs, and the five core explanations. Assign the range estimate, habitat challenge, or a published field comparison as desired.
Listen for
Students connect changes in the herd to access to food, survival and reproduction. Look for a clear distinction between simulated outcomes, historical estimates, and what the historical graph alone can establish.

Serengeti Board Game

Suggested time: 45–60 min; reserve the final 10–15 min for evidence and analysis

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
On one shared device, assign each role to a numbered player or team. For pairs, one player can take two roles. No names are needed. Solo mode lets one student play all three roles. Project a first turn if the class is new to the board. The original tabletop walkthrough plays inside How to play. The worked dice example opens before the first roll; compare the tie and unmatched die with the class.
Facilitate
Play from winter through fall, rotating who operates the device if useful. Flood cards say “lion pride clan token” in the original print; students remove a lion pride (P), not a hyena clan (C). Pause after one event or hunt so each role can check the turn record. Have groups cite a numbered event or hunt in Analyze. When their log has a pair with the same predator group size, ask how prey group size changes the starting dice and what one hunt cannot tell them.
Listen for
Students connect an actual play decision or event to their record. Cumulative change starts from zero; it is not a head count of the animals represented by the tokens. In Analyze, distinguish a one-year simulation from a claim about carrying capacity or actual population size.

Serengeti Ripple Effects

Suggested time: 15–20 min, after the Serengeti Board Game

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Connect a board-game observation to one local patch during a migration visit. Ask students to distinguish an influence arrow from a food-chain arrow.
Facilitate
Have students check the six links and predict a change in dung beetle activity. Compare the two runs at the same step. Ask students to trace at least two links and explain why grass can respond in opposite directions over time. The Explain panel keeps the same results beside the questions.
Listen for
Students distinguish indirect effects from direct ones, local encounters from predator population size, and simulated indices from field counts. The model changes visits, not total herd size. It does not predict the outcome of a real conservation action.

Start with observations of burning, investigate how peat forms and stores carbon, then trace carbon and energy through transfers.

Back to activity list

Burning Fuels: What Burns and Why

Suggested time: About 40 min

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Review what the BTB color, balance and thermometer each indicate. The entire online demonstration is simulated and requires no physical fuels.
Facilitate
Have students predict the temperature change, record before-and-after readings for the five fuels, and compare the evidence before explaining matter and energy changes. Use the saved grams lost, temperature change, and BTB color beside the questions in Make Sense. New simulated grass and leaf handfuls are about 4 g and 7 g; older saved simulations keep their original readings. Ask students what changes when sample mass changes, then have the class design a fairer comparison of fuel types. After students weigh the sample and read its temperature again, the site saves that burn automatically. Observation chips can still be added to the saved burn.
Listen for
Students distinguish observations from interpretations and note that the fuel samples start at unequal masses. Ask what comparing grams lost can tell them, and what would need to be held constant before generalizing.

Fires: Peat, Decomposition & Earth’s Tilt

Suggested time: About 60 min, or split into two shorter sessions

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Treat the Lesson 3 yeast investigation and Lesson 5 light investigation as separate assignments if that fits the class sequence.
Facilitate
For yeast, have students predict and compare foam over time in the cold, room-temperature and warm beakers. For the light investigation, compare the virtual BTB tubes across light conditions, then connect evidence from both investigations to the peat question. Put each group’s foam and tube readings beside its claims. If their setup does not include the requested comparison, use the labeled class example for discussion. Same-setting readings are saved, but they do not count as a comparison. The tilt readout shows differences in BTB score even when two tubes have the same color.
Listen for
Students name the changed variable and what they measured. They should distinguish foam as an indirect indicator, BTB as evidence about net CO₂ change, and both simulated investigations from direct measurements of an Arctic bog.

Peat Bog Carbon Lab

Suggested time: 45–60 min

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Choose one starting scenario and two variables for students to investigate separately. Use one device per student or pair and review the trial table before students start changing settings.
Facilitate
Record a baseline, then change one setting at a time and save labeled trials. Repeating the same settings does not count as a comparison. Compare the three process rates and the net carbon result. Have students use two graph values in Test one variable before previewing their final report. Use the winter fire or warming section as an extension rather than adding both variables to the same trial.
Listen for
Students support a claim with a matched comparison and distinguish the process rates from the net result. Model thresholds and rate units are simplified; they are not field measurements or universal cutoffs. Freezing stops modeled fire but can leave a small decomposition rate.

Carbon Atom Journey

Suggested time: 30–40 min for the dice journey; add 20–30 min for Carbon & Energy Budget

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Start with the original Lesson 9 dice journey. The optional Budget comparison unlocks after students finish the journey; returning students can still reach previously saved Budget work. Use one device per student or pair. For the journey, prepare a way to pool class counts if you want students to compare paths.
Facilitate
In Journey, record at least 10 rolls, counting stays. After repeated routes or stays, students read a reference card before rolling again. Finish checks for missed stations and decomposer evidence. A repeated Soil and rock stay can surface a separate seeded route showing Producers → Herbivores → Omnivores → Atmosphere. The stay streak remains a valid outcome of the official dice; the second route is selected as a contrast, not a frequency estimate. Reference cards do not count as rolls or change the atom’s path; students can revisit them beside question 3. Question 2 shows each student’s roll totals; a separate example is available for solo comparison in question 4. In Budget, advance the matched no-fire and peat-fire cases and use the two ledgers to support an explanation. Preview report includes both Journey and any saved Budget work. The Budget-only evidence download is for just that comparison.
Listen for
Students distinguish roll counts from elapsed time and keep carbon transfers separate from energy transfers. The paired fire case is a digital extension in model units, not an emissions or temperature forecast.

Cancer Rates Data Dive

Suggested time: 25–35 min in Lesson 1, solo or with a partner. Revisit the optional Michigan evidence in Lessons 9.5 and 12.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use after students share initial questions about who gets cancer and why. Explain that rates compare events with the size of the population; a rate is not a count. Begin with the national cancer-type and state views, and offer the state income/poverty and race/ethnicity views as extensions. No personal or family health information is needed.
Facilitate
Ask students to make a prediction before opening the evidence, sort and filter the table, and pin a comparison with two reported values. Have partners check that the comparison answers the submitted follow-up question (revisions are saved separately until submitted), then name both groups, values, units, and time period before they explain a pattern. In the state income/poverty view, name both study periods: U.S. Census 2023 poverty or median household income estimates and 2019–2023 cancer rates. Switch horizontal axes and ask if the same states stand out. The line summarizes the visible states; it cannot establish a causal effect. Ask what else they would need to investigate a possible cause. Have students select Finish investigation after the claim and limit are written, before turning in their download.
Evidence case file
Read the investigator briefing, then open each exhibit from the Evidence Folder. The case notebook keeps the student’s pinned evidence beside the final case report. Exhibits A–C preserve the original two 2021 rankings and state map. Use the age and height materials on the next day as planned. Exhibits D–F extend the discussion about access in Lesson 1 or Lesson 6.5. In D, cost blocked a needed visit for 29.3% of the lowest-income group shown versus 12.6% of the highest-income group shown. In E, 46.6% of cancer patients in neighborhoods with persistent poverty were diagnosed before the cancer spread beyond where it started, compared with 58.2% in other neighborhoods. This is the share of diagnoses across nine cancers in a California study, not the rate at which people develop cancer. Exhibit F uses a U.S. employer insurance change to compare higher versus lower annual deductibles (Wharam et al., 2019). Among women in higher-poverty neighborhoods, estimated delays were 1.6 months for diagnostic imaging, 2.7 for biopsy and 6.6 for early-stage diagnosis. These are group benchmark differences, not average waits from a symptom, and they do not add together. Different tests were not compared for accuracy or price; do not infer more late-stage diagnoses from this study. In the design task, ask students what affordable and reliable would each look like, and what their classroom prototype cannot prove. All six exhibits are required: students save their own observations for A–C and answer the checks in D–F correctly. Then they write a follow-up question to open the larger data collection. Saving a prediction opens the Evidence Folder rather than bypassing it. Their question and A–C observations stay visible through Explain.
Listen for
Students name the two groups and measure and cite comparable numbers. Distinguish diagnosis rates from death rates or survival. State patterns do not describe the economic circumstances of individuals, and national race/ethnicity labels do not identify genetic causes. In the race/ethnicity view (2018–2022), read the notes about how groups are defined and when coverage differs. Discuss how screening, reporting, exposure, and access can shape observed patterns, without claiming that these data isolate those effects.
Michigan investigation: screening, diagnosis & ultrasound design
Lesson 9.5 · Define the problem
Have partners compare Michigan counties on mammography uptake and late-stage breast cancer diagnosis. They should pin a comparison, check both measures’ units, time periods, eligible populations, and any missing data, then describe a problem the pattern suggests investigating. Have students compare urban and rural markers and switch the x-axis to median household income. Oakland and Lake support a possible income/access pattern; rural Osceola’s estimated mammography use exceeds urban Macomb’s, so it does not hold for every pair. County data cannot tell whether individuals who were screened were the same people diagnosed. Directory listings are not counts of appointments, training capacity, or equipment that is available to a particular patient.
Lesson 11.5 · Test the prototype
Collect cost, target visibility or image quality, and durability on training phantoms. Students can carry their measurements and access-directory findings forward in the optional ultrasound design notebook. Record the test conditions and units; leave untested measures blank rather than inventing results.
Lesson 12 · Review a design
Use the county comparison to explain why the problem matters and students’ own phantom results to evaluate the prototype. Keep these two kinds of evidence separate. A classroom phantom can demonstrate design trade-offs, not screening performance in people, improved clinical outcomes, or whether a device is safe for clinical use.

Cancer Screening Design Lab

Suggested time: 25–30 minutes, in pairs or solo, after the Cancer Rates Data Dive or alongside the modified B.3 engineering lessons.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Introduce all three case cards before running the regional hospital plan. Then move the ultrasound machine and trained sonographer as one team, keeping screening at the hospital. Frame this as an engineering comparison, not a clinical decision. Keep patient travel and test costs separate from the network’s equipment and staff expenses.
Facilitate
Start with three randomized fictional patients, one from each town. In the Design and test tab, run the hospital plan, then record a redesign and compare the same three cases. The practice booking is separate from the recorded district design. After all three checks, open Recommend a plan to write and finish. Each correct check fills a row on the decision board. Changing designs keeps previous comparisons when the correct answer stays the same. Patient numbers usually differ from student to student, so compare reasoning rather than expecting identical results. The visible Dataset ID lets students confirm they are working on the same saved district. Practice wallets are separate by patient and plan, and the cost sheet explains fees, time away, drive cost, and the voucher cap. The mission badge requires a plan within 720 credits, a net increase in patients who reach all needed care across the same 30-person district, and all three featured patients reaching needed care. Fresh cases include a higher-income patient who can complete both hospital visits and two lower-income patients whose hospital pathway costs more than they can afford. At least one plan helps all three without exceeding the clinic budget. Older saves keep their original cases and scoring. Fresh districts always have at least one winning redesign. An older saved district with no possible improvement can earn a best-attainable result without resetting. Checking all three cases and explaining a trade-off completes the investigation even if a design does not win. Discuss any patients who gained or lost access, not just the net change. Have pairs open a patient’s cost sheet and try booking both required visits without running out of credits or time. Ask them to distinguish a service fee from the drive expense and the model’s value for time away. Compare one-way distances before explaining differences. After the core comparison, optionally try local screening or ride vouchers. Open the practice scan to show how a simulated mass can appear in one slice and not in another. Mark the image, then ask what it cannot tell you. Ask which expense or constraint changed. Vouchers reduce a model travel expense, not travel hours. Ask what a grant for a machine would and would not solve.
Listen for
Students compare the same three patients in both plans and distinguish the network’s cost from the patient’s travel and test burden. The report records the three cases, both plans, and written recommendations. Evidence stays beside the writing. On a phone, open View my plan and patient data to check the same comparison without returning to Design and test.
Model limits
All cases and credits are invented. Ultrasound is a follow-up imaging tool for selected modeled cases, not a universal screening test or a cancer diagnosis. Lower patient spending may reflect more missed visits. Do not infer clinical performance, real clinic prices, or patient wealth from location. This model does not replace the hands-on ultrasound phantom investigation.

Cell Cycle & Cancer

Suggested time: About 40 min for selected sections; allow a second period for full play and growth-model analysis

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Choose the short route or the full two-period investigation before class.
Facilitate · short route · about 40 min
Skip the game. Open the Growth model, write a prediction, and run Sets 1 and 2 below. In Analyze, assign Q1 and Q2 (Part 1), Q4a (Part 2), and one model limitation (Part 4). Skip the other prompts. In Wrap up, use the submission report. It may be marked in progress because this route assigns only the growth portion.
Full route · period 1
Use solo play or groups of two to four sharing a device. Compare the round with working p53 checkpoints with the Cellular Chance round. Students can play both rounds or compare recorded class results. Save one checkpoint event to cite in Analyze.
Full route · period 2
Run each of the four growth-model settings below, then complete Analyze. Each set records three simulated trials. Open View my data at an Analyze question on a phone for its recorded results. Use the same baseline for all three comparisons; change only one setting at a time.
  1. Baseline: Skin, age 50, average body size.
  2. Age: Skin, age 70, average body size. Compare with Set 1 for Q4a.
  3. Body size: Skin, age 50, larger body size. Compare with Set 1 for Q4b.
  4. Cell type: Intestinal lining, age 50, average body size. Compare with Set 1 for Q3.
Listen for
Students cite a checkpoint event and compare repeated growth-model trials rather than a single outcome. Model results are not predictions for an individual.

DNA Workshop: Build, Copy, Decode

Suggested time: 20–30 min before DNA Copying, p53 & Gene Expression. No prior DNA knowledge or equipment required.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Students first assemble one nucleotide from a phosphate, a sugar, and a base, then use one short fictional example through the molecule-modeling stations. Drag the diagram pieces, or select a piece and then its slot by touch or keyboard. The pairing and codon keys stay with the work.
Facilitate
Ask students to point to the phosphate and the base attaching to the sugar. At Copy, ask them to point to the original strand in each DNA molecule. At Make RNA (transcription), have them point to the one DNA template. At Build a Chain (translation), students decode a fresh fictional RNA message with eight amino acids and a stop codon. Ask how AUG both starts translation and adds methionine, and why Stop adds no amino acid.
Listen for
Copying DNA, making RNA, and building an amino acid chain make different products. The chain is not a fully folded, working protein. This model omits many molecules and steps. Written observations are optional. Use the final sort or downloaded work as a quick readiness check, then open the next activity from the last screen.

DNA Copying, p53 & Gene Expression

Suggested time: 60–75 min, or two sessions split by lesson

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Plan a pause between the Lesson 5 DNA copying model and the Lesson 6 gene expression model.
Facilitate
Have students record DNA fork evidence before and after version a. If you assign the optional versions b and c, try them before a repairs all mismatches. Then complete the three distinct required expression strands (A, B, and C) and revise an explanation. For a one-period selection, tell students to submit a draft. Peer feedback can support the revision but is optional.
Listen for
Students distinguish a copying error from a lasting mutation and connect changes in a gene to changes in a protein. Treat p53 as part of the DNA-damage response; the simplified model does not represent every repair pathway.

Hawksbeard Wind Lab

Suggested time: 20–30 min for the digital investigation; add discussion time for the published study.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Use this as the no-materials option or as a follow-up to the physical seed dispersal test. Explain that the paired releases keep seed numbers and paths the same. Use Enlarge boards for a closer look at seed landings on phones or the projector.
Facilitate
Ask students to predict before releasing five matched plant pairs. Have partners compare runs with different Dataset IDs. Within a trial, both layouts get the same paths; a new trial draws different paths. The starting prediction is kept once seeds are released. In Compare, revisit it with a fraction and name the denominator. The written comparison unlocks Explain; the optional denominator check is included in student reports when answered. Rat Generations

Suggested time: 30–40 min for the digital model and explanation; add time for class discussion of the readings.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use this as the no-materials alternative to the Lesson 3 bead model. Students drag the two assigned rats across the illustrated burrow to their nest, then build the assigned Punnett squares with reusable R and B pieces. Identical alleles are interchangeable; the positions on the square still show which allele each parent contributes to an expected offspring. They can use a mouse, touch, or the keyboard. Recorded crosses can be inspected without having to repeat them. This classroom model does not involve live animals or pest-control procedures.
Facilitate
Use the burrow and timeline to compare births with the survivors of Gen 3 and Gen 4. The stage shows which rats stayed in the modeled population and which are no longer counted. Gen 5 ends at births. Have partners compare Dataset IDs and look for how different initial pairings affected the record. Before producing a new generation, students build a square for every assigned pair. Saved generations are not sent back to earlier work. In Analyze, Try chance alone independently samples two litters from the same parents, without changing the lesson’s expected counts or the report. Students use the quick-check feedback to correct the denominator before opening Explain. The report retains a saved attempt while it is still in progress.
Listen for
Keep colony size, fraction of rats showing the trait, and R allele frequency separate. Ask why every survivor can carry R even though B is still in the group. Reinforce that selection changes which existing individuals reproduce, not which alleles they already have. The Tokyo observation is not a model output or a target. Selection starts after Generation 3 births to give students a before-and-after comparison, not because that generation is biologically special. Real litters can differ by chance from a square’s expected outcomes; that chance can shift allele frequencies in small populations (genetic drift).

Junco Investigation

Suggested time: 25–35 min for the Lesson 4 digital investigation and partner discussion.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use after students revisit the city and mountain junco observations. Ask what a shorter distance before flying away would mean for an approaching person. Introduce CORT as a hormone the researchers measured.
Facilitate
In Field, optionally run the animated walk-up experiment. Keep the observation context and step size the same when comparing city and mountain birds; run more than one trial per population. The simulation uses the published group means to set a general pattern, but its bird-to-bird variation and trial outcomes are illustrative, not research data. Then compare the published field observations and write a field note to unlock the aviary. Before revealing the aviary results, use the cutaway to identify what was matched: housing, food and exposure to people. Play the sample care routine to see both dishes refilled. The feeding steps and birds hopping toward the dishes are illustrations of matched care, not records of the actual care protocol, facility or measured behavior. With reduced motion, advance the same routine one still frame at a time. Ask what each explanation predicts for exploration of the new room, which is a different behavior from field takeoff. In CORT, have students read one dot aloud: the higher of the hormone levels measured 15 and 30 minutes after capture and the same bird’s score from a separate exploration test. Use the field and aviary summaries by the final response, rather than treating either one as a complete explanation. On a phone, open View CORT graph and data or View field and aviary evidence to revisit those sources without leaving the report step. In the optional virtual fieldwork, select Change setup to abandon an unfinished approach without deleting earlier trials or using up a trial number. Both file formats use the same Dataset ID and include the full published evidence as it is unlocked, separate from virtual trials.
Listen for
The aviary study controls many differences in living conditions; a difference that persists supports an inherited contribution but does not rule out effects before capture. CORT and boldness are related in the data, not proof that hormone levels caused the behavior. The scatterplot coordinates were digitized from the Lesson 4 graph and are approximate. That graph does not identify each bird’s population, so it cannot be used for a within-population comparison. The linked OpenSciEd Junco Data Excursion offers a Location field for that comparison. Have students separate individual behavior from changes across generations.

Wildlife Corridor Lab

Suggested time: 25–35 min for this Lessons 7–9 companion. Start with the Florida panther range and genetic evidence; then test how habitat connectivity changes inheritance.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Compare the historical range with the recent breeding area. Ask students which observations about survival and reproduction their natural selection model explains, and why it needs more evidence to account for low genetic diversity. Keep the Lesson 7 reading and discussion; the digital note can travel into Explain. Then have students read A/A, A/a, and a/a as two inherited copies of one gene. A is dominant for fictional coat appearance only; neither allele affects survival. Then have students predict how the road will affect the difference in allele counts. Call gene flow the movement of gene versions between populations when migrants reproduce.
Facilitate
Run the connected and road-split comparison. Place a crossing by dragging or by selecting it and choosing the marked location; run that comparison from the same starting populations. Ask students to choose an offspring and watch the two sampled parents meet on the map and each pass one copy, then compare the gap in A counts between the two sides using the recorded results. Have them explain their conclusion and name a limit before using Preview report.
Listen for
A crossing can let animals move and reproduce on the other side, allowing gene versions to mix. An animal moving alone is not enough to demonstrate gene flow. Similar frequencies across habitats do not automatically mean greater diversity within both habitats. The model uses fictional diploid populations (two copies of the gene per individual), fixed population sizes, and two alleles that do not change survival or reproduction. It is not an estimate of real crossing use, population growth, or genetic outcomes.

Three Bears in Wapusk

Suggested time: 25–35 minutes of digital work spread across the original Lesson 1 sequence. Keep the partner talk, Inuvialuit evidence, fishbowl discussion, paper models, and Driving Question Board.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Play the original Wapusk video if available. With or without it, have students switch seasons and compare the interactive schematic with the official sighting maps. Use the original Data Exploration slides and Scale Organizer before the regional inquiry.
Facilitate
Assign regions as in the original lesson. The three uploaded regional resources provide the 2018 assessment snapshots. For Islands Ice, use the transcript or video and bring in the original regional deck when available. Have students compare a seasonal-ice and a persistent-ice model and bring one question to the class board.
Listen for
Shared space is not evidence of an encounter, and an unreported sighting is not proof of absence. Keep the four-female telemetry sample separate from habitat markers. Insufficient data do not show stable reproduction or body condition. No classroom data entry is shared automatically.
Read the scales
The Arctic chart plots annual minimum extent from NSIDC, not the matching NASA animation’s measure of ice area. In the regional snapshot, short-term population size, body condition and reproduction are separate evidence, not competing estimates of the same outcome. The Owl River and scale check verdicts carry into all report formats, alongside Parks Canada’s 11,475 km² figure for the park itself. The sightings maps include the surrounding area, too.

Listen for
Students link inherited variation, different environments, survival and reproduction over many generations. A fossil gives a minimum age for presence, not an exact split date or location.
