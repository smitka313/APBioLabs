Unit 1 · Ecosystem Interactions & Dynamics

Trace a seasonal pattern first, then test a resource limit and use the board game to investigate how migration changes predator encounters.

Back to activity list

Migration Map Explorer

Suggested time: 40–50 min for selected questions; two periods for the full investigation

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Decide which months and question sets students will investigate; the activity covers material from both Lessons 3 and 4.
Facilitate
Start with a seasonal map comparison and a prediction. Then group the health cards by month and compare the same measure across groups. Assign an explanation set and have students open its source evidence beside their draft.
Listen for
Students identify the month, group and measure they are citing. Ask them to distinguish the schematic map, monthly group averages, long-term rainfall averages and the single example year of occupancy.

Carrying Capacity

Suggested time: 45–55 min for the grass model and explanation; add 25–35 min for either extension

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Decide whether to assign the Lesson 5 grass model only, or also the separate Habitat Challenge or historical population investigation.
Facilitate
Have students predict, run the baseline, and repeat with one change in grass availability. Compare late-round patterns rather than just final counts. For an extension, choose either a matched habitat comparison or an interval from the published field estimates.
Listen for
Students connect changes in the herd to access to food, survival and reproduction. Look for a clear distinction between simulated outcomes, historical estimates, and what the historical graph alone can establish.

Serengeti Board Game

Suggested time: 45–60 min; reserve the final 10–15 min for evidence and analysis

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one shared device for each group. Assign the wildebeest, hyena and lion roles to three students or teams. Project a first turn if the class is new to the board.
Facilitate
Play from winter through fall, rotating who operates the device if useful. Pause after one event or hunt so each role can check the turn record. Have groups compare seasons and cite an event or hunt in Analyze.
Listen for
Students connect an actual play decision or event to their record. Cumulative change starts from zero; it is not a head count of the animals represented by the tokens.

Unit 2 · Ecosystems: Matter & Energy

Start with observations of burning, investigate how peat forms and stores carbon, then trace carbon and energy through transfers.

Back to activity list

Burning Fuels: What Burns and Why

Suggested time: About 40 min

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Review what the BTB color, balance and thermometer each indicate. The entire online demonstration is simulated and requires no physical fuels.
Facilitate
Have students predict, record before-and-after readings for the five fuels, and compare evidence before explaining the matter and energy changes. Use a class discussion to design a fairer fuel comparison.
Listen for
Students distinguish observations from interpretations and note that the fuel samples start at unequal masses. Ask what comparing grams lost can tell them, and what would need to be held constant before generalizing.

Fires: Peat, Decomposition & Earth’s Tilt

Suggested time: About 60 min, or split into two shorter sessions

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Treat the Lesson 3 yeast investigation and Lesson 5 light investigation as separate assignments if that fits the class sequence.
Facilitate
For yeast, have students predict and compare foam over time in the cold, room-temperature and warm beakers. For the light investigation, compare the virtual BTB tubes across light conditions, then connect evidence from both investigations to the peat question.
Listen for
Students name the changed variable and what they measured. They should distinguish foam as an indirect indicator, BTB as evidence about net CO₂ change, and both simulated investigations from direct measurements of an Arctic bog.

Peat Bog Carbon Lab

Suggested time: 45–60 min

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Choose one starting scenario and two variables for students to investigate separately. Use one device per student or pair and review the trial table before students start changing settings.
Facilitate
Record a baseline, then change one setting at a time and save labeled trials. Compare the three process rates and the net carbon result. Use the winter fire or warming section as an extension rather than adding both variables to the same trial.
Listen for
Students support a claim with a matched comparison and distinguish the process rates from the net result. Model thresholds and rate units are simplified; they are not field measurements or universal cutoffs.

Carbon Atom Journey

Suggested time: 30–40 min for the dice journey; add 20–30 min for Carbon & Energy Budget

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Choose the original Lesson 9 dice journey, the optional Budget comparison, or both. Use one device per student or pair. For the journey, prepare a way to pool class counts if you want students to compare paths.
Facilitate
In Journey, record at least 10 rolls and compare journals, counting turns that stay at a station. In Budget, advance the matched no-fire and peat-fire cases and use the two ledgers to support an explanation.
Listen for
Students distinguish roll counts from elapsed time and keep carbon transfers separate from energy transfers. The paired fire case is a digital extension in model units, not an emissions or temperature forecast.

Unit 3 · Inheritance & Variation of Traits

Start with patterns in cancer data, move from cell division and growth to DNA and gene expression, then weigh inherited and environmental evidence without treating risk as certainty.

Back to activity list

Cancer Rates Data Dive

Suggested time: 25–35 min in Lesson 1, solo or with a partner. Revisit the optional Michigan evidence in Lessons 9.5 and 12.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use after students share initial questions about who gets cancer and why. Explain that rates compare events with the size of the population; a rate is not a count. Begin with the national cancer-type and state views, and offer the state income/poverty and race/ethnicity views as extensions. No personal or family health information is needed.
Facilitate
Ask students to make a prediction before opening the evidence, sort and filter the table, and pin a comparison. Have partners check the measure, unit, groups, and time period before they explain a pattern. In the state income/poverty view, name both study periods: U.S. Census 2023 poverty or median household income estimates and 2019–2023 cancer rates. Switch horizontal axes and ask if the same states stand out. The line summarizes the visible states; it cannot establish a causal effect. Ask what else they would need to investigate a possible cause.
Evidence case file
Read the investigator briefing, then open each exhibit from the Evidence Folder. The case notebook keeps the student’s pinned evidence beside the final case report. Exhibits A–C preserve the original two 2021 rankings and state map. Use the age and height materials on the next day as planned. Exhibits D–F extend the discussion about access in Lesson 1 or Lesson 6.5. In D, cost blocked a needed visit for 29.3% of the lowest-income group shown versus 12.6% of the highest-income group shown. In E, 46.6% of cancer patients in neighborhoods with persistent poverty were diagnosed before the cancer spread beyond where it started, compared with 58.2% in other neighborhoods. This is the share of diagnoses across nine cancers in a California study, not the rate at which people develop cancer. Exhibit F uses a U.S. employer insurance change to compare higher versus lower annual deductibles (Wharam et al., 2019). Among women in higher-poverty neighborhoods, estimated delays were 1.6 months for diagnostic imaging, 2.7 for biopsy and 6.6 for early-stage diagnosis. These are group benchmark differences, not average waits from a symptom, and they do not add together. Different tests were not compared for accuracy or price; do not infer more late-stage diagnoses from this study. In the design task, ask students what affordable and reliable would each look like, and what their classroom prototype cannot prove. All six exhibits are required: students save their own observations for A–C and answer the checks in D–F correctly. Then they write a follow-up question to open the larger data collection. Saving a prediction opens the Evidence Folder rather than bypassing it. Their question and A–C observations stay visible through Explain.
Listen for
Students name the two groups and measure and cite comparable numbers. Distinguish diagnosis rates from death rates or survival. State patterns do not describe the economic circumstances of individuals, and national race/ethnicity labels do not identify genetic causes. In the race/ethnicity view (2018–2022), read the notes about how groups are defined and when coverage differs. Discuss how screening, reporting, exposure, and access can shape observed patterns, without claiming that these data isolate those effects.
Michigan investigation: screening, diagnosis & ultrasound design
Lesson 9.5 · Define the problem
Have partners compare Michigan counties on mammography uptake and late-stage breast cancer diagnosis. They should pin a comparison, check both measures’ units, time periods, eligible populations, and any missing data, then describe a problem the pattern suggests investigating. Have students compare urban and rural markers and switch the x-axis to median household income. Oakland and Lake support a possible income/access pattern; rural Osceola’s estimated mammography use exceeds urban Macomb’s, so it does not hold for every pair. County data cannot tell whether individuals who were screened were the same people diagnosed. Directory listings are not counts of appointments, training capacity, or equipment that is available to a particular patient.
Lesson 11.5 · Test the prototype
Collect cost, target visibility or image quality, and durability on training phantoms. Students can carry their measurements and access-directory findings forward in the optional ultrasound design notebook. Record the test conditions and units; leave untested measures blank rather than inventing results.
Lesson 12 · Review a design
Use the county comparison to explain why the problem matters and students’ own phantom results to evaluate the prototype. Keep these two kinds of evidence separate. A classroom phantom can demonstrate design trade-offs, not screening performance in people, improved clinical outcomes, or whether a device is safe for clinical use.

Cell Cycle & Cancer

Suggested time: About 40 min for selected sections; allow a second period for full play and growth-model analysis

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Choose solo play or groups of two to four sharing a device. Decide whether students will play both rounds themselves or compare class results. If using the growth model, pick one comparison variable.
Facilitate
Compare the round with working p53 checkpoints with the Cellular Chance round. Have students cite recorded gameplay before discussing model limits. In the growth model, run three trials per setting and change one variable at a time.
Listen for
Students cite a checkpoint or event and use repeated growth-model evidence, not a single outcome. Discuss how the game simplifies cell-cycle regulation and why model results are not predictions for an individual.

DNA Workshop: Build, Copy, Decode

Suggested time: 15–20 min before DNA Copying, p53 & Gene Expression. No prior DNA knowledge or equipment required.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Students first assemble one nucleotide from a phosphate, a sugar, and a base, then use one short fictional example through the molecule-modeling stations. Drag the diagram pieces, or select a piece and then its slot by touch or keyboard. The pairing and codon keys stay with the work.
Facilitate
Ask students to point to the phosphate and the base attaching to the sugar. At Copy, ask them to point to the original strand in each DNA molecule. At Make RNA, have them point to the one DNA template. At Build a Chain, ask what the ribosome is reading and why the stop signal adds no amino acid.
Listen for
Copying DNA, making RNA, and building an amino acid chain make different products. The chain is not a fully folded, working protein. This model omits many molecules and steps. Use the final sort or downloaded work as a quick readiness check, then open the next activity from the last screen.

DNA Copying, p53 & Gene Expression

Suggested time: 60–75 min, or two sessions split by lesson

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Plan a pause between the Lesson 5 DNA copying model and the Lesson 6 gene expression model.
Facilitate
Have students compare DNA fork outcomes and note where mismatches were found. Then have them try all three strands in the gene expression model and revise an explanation using the displayed evidence.
Listen for
Students distinguish a copying error from a lasting mutation and connect changes in a gene to changes in a protein. Treat p53 as part of the DNA-damage response; the simplified model does not represent every repair pathway.

Inheritance, Environment & Cancer Risk

Suggested time: 60–80 min, or two sessions split by lesson

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Preview the note about cancer and family history. Use the activity’s cases; students do not need to share personal or family health information.
Facilitate
Start with the pedigree and meiosis models to compare possible inherited allele combinations. In the second session, compare skin cancer graphs and design a controlled comparison in the virtual UV-and-yeast investigation.
Listen for
Students distinguish inherited susceptibility, environmental exposure and chance from a prediction for any individual. Ask what the yeast comparison measures directly and what it cannot establish about human cancer.

Unit 4 · Natural Selection & Evolution of Populations

Start by testing how city landscapes change where seeds can land. Later investigations connect such differences to reproduction and population change.

Back to activity list

Hawksbeard Wind Lab

Suggested time: 20–30 min for the digital investigation; add discussion time for the published study.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use one device per student or pair. Use this as the no-materials option or as a follow-up to the physical seed dispersal test. Explain that the paired releases keep seed numbers and paths the same. Use Enlarge boards for a closer look at seed landings on phones or the projector.
Facilitate
Ask students to predict before releasing five matched plant pairs. Have partners compare runs with different Dataset IDs. Within a trial, both layouts get the same paths; a new trial draws different paths. In Compare, students revisit their prediction with a fraction. From Explain, they can add a name and class period directly to the downloaded report, without using the dashboard first. Use the optional published study to compare kinds of evidence.
Listen for
Have students name the denominator in each claim and distinguish a landing on soil from germination. This model changes soil amount and arrangement together and does not model future generations.

Rat Generations

Suggested time: 25–35 min for the digital model and explanation; add time for class discussion of the readings.

Open activity
Classroom plan
Set up
Use this as the no-materials alternative to the Lesson 3 bead model. Students drag the two assigned rats across the illustrated burrow to their nest, then build one Punnett square with reusable R and B pieces. Identical alleles are interchangeable; the positions on the square still show which allele each parent contributes to an expected offspring. They can use a mouse, touch, or the keyboard. Later crosses can be built for practice or inspected without repeating the full exercise. This classroom model does not involve live animals or pest-control procedures.
Facilitate
Use the burrow and timeline to compare births with the survivors of Gen 3 and Gen 4. The stage shows which rats stayed in the modeled population and which are no longer counted. Gen 5 ends at births. Have partners compare Dataset IDs and look for how different initial pairings affected the record.
Listen for
Keep colony size, fraction of rats showing the trait, and R allele frequency separate. Ask why every survivor can carry R even though B is still in the group. Reinforce that selection changes which existing individuals reproduce, not which alleles they already have. The Tokyo observation is not a model output or a target.
