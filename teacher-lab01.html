Artificial Selection

Can extreme selection change expression of a quantitative trait in a population in one generation?

Unit 7: Natural SelectionEVO-1.FEVO-1.GScience Practice 4.B
    At a glance

    Summary for planning

    Timing (wet lab)
    About 7 weeks: one Fast Plant generation seed to seed, plus about 10 days of growth for generation 2. Most days need only 5–10 minutes (watering, notes). Longer sessions: planting, scoring and selecting, pollinating (days 14–16), scoring generation 2.
    Timing (online)
    About 2 class periods: one for background, pre-lab, scoring and selection; one for generation 2, analysis and the design activity. The student-designed part can extend as long as you like.
    Placement
    Works well at the start of the year or to introduce natural selection (Unit 7). Students need little prior content.
    Enduring understanding
    EVO-1: Evolution is characterized by a change in the genetic makeup of a population over time and is supported by multiple lines of evidence.
    Learning objectives
    EVO-1.F (how humans affect diversity within a population); EVO-1.G (environmental change and evolutionary change).
    Science practice
    4.B Describe data from a table or graph. Also exercises descriptive statistics, histograms, SE error bars and a two-sample t-test.
    Preparation

    Real materials and setup

    • Seed: Wisconsin Fast Plants (Brassica rapa). Stock C1-122 is recommended: it varies in hairiness and is heterozygous for two Mendelian traits (green/light-green leaf; with/without anthocyanin), so the unselected 90% can produce an F2 dihybrid population for a later genetics lab. Standard Fast Plant seed also works.
    • Growing system: recycled 1-L (or smaller) soda/water bottles converted to wick-watered planters with mason twine, Jiffy-Mix soil mix (not potting soil), vermiculite, and dilute fertilizer (about one bottle cap of Miracle-Gro Nursery Select or Peters Professional per 8 L of water). Up to 6 plants per bottle.
    • Lights: a light box or homemade light rack (plans on the Fast Plants website). Two separate light areas are needed so the selected population can be isolated during pollination.
    • Other: bee sticks (separate sticks for selected and unselected plants), plastic magnifiers, plastic plant stakes (cut from milk jugs, 1 cm × 10 cm) to record counts, digital cameras, lab notebooks.
    • Population size: 120–180 plants per class. Selecting the top 10% then gives 12–18 parents, enough to make seed.
    • Logistics: start on a Monday or Tuesday, fill reservoirs before weekends, and keep each class's plants clearly labeled. Soapy water, insecticidal soap, horticultural oil or diatomaceous earth control common pests.
    • Safety: keep water and fertilizer away from light fixtures and cords.
    How the simulation maps to the bench

    What the online version does, and its limits

    • Steps 1–5 (grow and score): 150 seedlings in 25 bottles of six. Students open each plant's first true leaf in a magnifier view and tap the hairs on the right margin (the manual's suggested sampling rule). Each hand count is compared with a "recount," which opens a discussion about counting fidelity. Auto-count scores the rest. The page reports n, mean, median, range and SD and draws a histogram with selectable bin widths (2, 5 or 10 hairs), echoing Chapter 3's advice to try several bin sizes.
    • Steps 6–10 (select and pollinate): students choose direction and fraction (5–20%, default 10%). Unselected plants are shown with their tops cut. An animated bee stick visits every flower of every selected plant over three days. A checkbox also grows a control line from the unselected 90%, as in Steps 10–11.
    • Steps 11–14 (generation 2): generation 2 is grown from random pairs of different selected parents (self-incompatibility). Students compare histograms, a bar graph of means with ±2 SE bars, a Welch t statistic, and calculate S, R and realized heritability themselves. Further rounds of selection can be run.
    • Design: students choose trait (trichomes, height, anthocyanin score), direction, selection intensity, population size, generations, replicate lines, uneven growing conditions and herbivore damage. Each run includes a control line bred from randomly chosen parents of the same number.
    The model

    Each trait is additive and polygenic. Trichomes: 24 unlinked loci, "+" allele frequency 0.30, each + allele adds 2.2 hairs, environmental SD 7 hairs, giving VG ≈ VE and h² ≈ 0.5. Counts are floored at zero, so the distribution is right-skewed with some hairless plants. Height h² ≈ 0.3 (mean ≈ 10 cm); anthocyanin score h² ≈ 0.6 (mean ≈ 4 on a 0–10 scale). Inheritance is Mendelian with independent assortment. "Uneven conditions" doubles environmental SD (lowering h²). Herbivore damage is a non-heritable plastic response (trichomes ×1.4 + 3; height −12%; anthocyanin +1.5).

    Limits: no linkage, dominance, epistasis, genotype-by-environment interaction or maternal effects; no inbreeding depression or seed-set failure; plants never die. Every plant is scored at exactly the same stage. The "recount" is treated as the true value, while real recounts also have error. Seven weeks of plant care, observation and notebook work cannot be reproduced online; use the simulation to prepare for, supplement or replace the wet lab when growing is not possible.

    Common misconceptions

    Watch for these ideas

    • "The offspring will be as hairy as their parents." Only the heritable part of the parents' advantage is passed on (R = h²S), so generation 2 moves only part of the way toward the parents' mean.
    • "Individual plants evolved." Individuals do not change; the population's distribution shifts because of who reproduced.
    • "Selection creates new hairy variants." Selection sorts existing variation. A large response depends on genetic variation already present (in the model, + alleles at frequency 0.3).
    • "Artificial selection is not real evolution." It meets the definition: a change in the genetic makeup of a population over generations. Only the agent of selection differs.
    • "A higher mean proves the effect." Without a measure of variation (SD, SE) or a test, a difference could be chance. The control line also shows how much the mean drifts without selection.
    • "Herbivore damage makes the next generation hairier." Induced trichomes are plastic, not inherited. In the design tool, herbivory raises every generation equally but does not accumulate.
    Suggested answers

    Pre-lab and expected results

    Pre-lab

    1. Varies among individuals; at least partly heritable.
    2. Number of trichomes on the first true leaf margin.
    3. 15 plants.
    4. S = 20 hairs; R = 10 hairs; predicted mean 22 hairs.
    5. Median (2 hairs). The outlier inflates the mean.
    6. Self-incompatibility, and pollen from unselected plants would dilute selection.
    7. SE = 9.0 ÷ 12 = 0.75 hairs.

    Typical simulation results (one round, top 10%)

    • Generation 1: mean ≈ 11–13 hairs, median similar or slightly lower, SD ≈ 8–9, range about 0–40, a few percent of plants with 0 hairs. The histogram is right-skewed, as in the manual's Figure 1.
    • Selected parents (15 plants): mean ≈ 26–30 hairs, SD ≈ 3–4.
    • Generation 2, selected line: mean ≈ 19–23 hairs, SD ≈ 8–10, distribution shifted right (manual's Figure 2). Welch t is typically 6–11, far above the critical value (~1.97), so the difference is significant.
    • Generation 2, control line: mean slightly below generation 1 (≈ 10–11) because the hairiest plants were removed from its parents.
    • Realized h² = R ÷ S is usually 0.4–0.7 (true value ≈ 0.5); the spread from class to class is itself a good discussion point about sampling only 15 parents.
    • Continued selection keeps raising the mean by roughly 6–9 hairs per generation for several generations. Selecting for fewer hairs responds more weakly because many plants pile up at zero (a floor effect).

    Analysis questions

    • Different? Yes: the generation 2 histogram is shifted toward more hairs and its mean and median are higher.
    • Significant? ±2 SE bars do not overlap and the t-test rejects the null hypothesis of equal means.
    • Median or mean? Trichome counts are skewed with a floor at zero, so the median is a robust summary; the mean is needed for SE, t-tests and the breeder's equation. Students should justify their choice.
    • Error bars: ±2 SE (approximate 95% CI of the mean). SD bars describe spread among plants, not confidence in the mean.
    • Gen 2 vs selected parents: generation 2 is much less extreme than its parents (regression toward the mean) because only part of the parents' superiority was genetic. This comparison estimates heritability; comparing generation 2 with all of generation 1 measures the response.
    • Did evolution occur? Yes: allele frequencies at hair-number genes changed between generations, shown by a heritable shift in the distribution under identical growing conditions.
    Student-designed investigations

    Ideas that work

    • Up versus down selection on trichomes (asymmetric response).
    • Selection intensity: 5% vs 20% vs 50% selected, with the same number of generations.
    • Heritability comparison: select height (low h²) versus anthocyanin (high h²).
    • Environmental variance: uniform versus uneven growing conditions; how much of the variation is environmental?
    • Plasticity: does early herbivore damage raise trichome counts, and does the effect carry over to the next generation?
    • Replicate lines: how repeatable is the response, and why do replicate lines diverge?
    • Wet-lab follow-ups from the manual: do trichomes deter cabbage white butterfly (Pieris rapae) larvae or egg laying; does early damage induce more trichomes on later leaves; can students quantify purple color reliably and select on it?
    Extensions and assessment

    Going further

    • Use C1-122 seed so the unselected plants produce an F2 dihybrid generation for a chi-square genetics lab (the Fast Plants "Who's the Father?" activity).
    • Have students present miniposters with peer review using your rubric, then revise before final evaluation; display them publicly.
    • Read the literature listed in the manual (for example, Ågren and Schemske's studies of trichome cost and evolution in Brassica rapa) and compare their heritability estimates with the class's realized h².
    • Connect to inadvertent selection in the real world: antibiotic and pesticide resistance (extreme selection, rapid change, preexisting variation).
