Mathematical Modeling: Hardy-Weinberg

How can mathematical models be used to investigate the relationship between allele frequencies in populations of organisms and evolutionary change?

Unit 7: Natural SelectionEVO-1.KEVO-1.LScience Practice 5.A
    At a glance

    Summary for planning

    Timing
    Two class periods to build and test the one-generation model, at least one more for multi-generation exploration and student questions. Add a period if you follow up with the supertaster lab.
    Placement
    After Mendelian genetics, as students begin evolution (Unit 7). Also fits genetics (Big Idea 3).
    Enduring understanding
    EVO-1: Evolution is characterized by a change in the genetic makeup of a population over time and is supported by multiple lines of evidence.
    Learning objectives
    EVO-1.K (conditions under which allele and genotype frequencies change); EVO-1.L (impacts when Hardy-Weinberg conditions are not met).
    Science practice
    5.A Perform mathematical calculations (p + q = 1, p² + 2pq + q² = 1, chi-square).
    Prerequisites
    Genes vs alleles, Punnett squares, basic probability. Spreadsheet IF statements are the main hurdle; the page shows every formula so students can rebuild it in a real spreadsheet.
    Preparation

    Materials

    • Computers or tablets with a browser (this page). For the full manual experience, a spreadsheet program (Excel, Numbers, LibreOffice Calc or Google Sheets) so students build the model themselves.
    • Lab notebooks; miniposter supplies for the summative presentation.
    • Optional physical model: decks of index cards marked A and B (four per student) for the card simulation, or the "M&M" activity.
    • Optional advanced models named in the manual: Deme 1.0/2.0 (ESTEEM, Excel), AlleleA1 (Jon Herron) and the Radford population genetics simulation. Check that links still work before class.
    • Work through the model build yourself first; the manual's author recommends it as the most important preparation.
    Classroom management

    Have students save versions of spreadsheet models under new names as they go. On this page, tables and written answers save in each student's browser; students copy everything with the Submit button. Because finished H-W spreadsheets are easy to find online, have the building done in class.

    How the simulation maps to the manual

    What the online version does, and its limits

    • Card simulation (Getting Started): a classic pencil-and-paper model. Students start as AB, mate randomly, each partner passes one random allele per offspring, two offspring per pair. Case I ideal; II BB lethal; III BB lethal and AA 50% survival (heterozygote advantage); IV isolated groups of 4–6 (drift). Small class sizes show why the manual warns that classroom simulations are dominated by drift.
    • Build the model: an in-page spreadsheet laid out exactly as in the manual's Figures 2–8: p in D2, =1-D2 in D3, gametes in E and F using =IF(RAND()<=D$2,"A","B"), zygotes via CONCATENATE in G, genotype bookkeeping in H, I (nested IF for AB/BA) and J, SUM totals, frequencies and new p. Clicking a cell shows its formula. Students recalculate, change p and the number of rows, and record runs. A bar graph compares observed counts with p²N, 2pqN, q²N, and a chi-square (df = 1, using p̂ from the data) checks HW proportions.
    • Many generations: the manual's Figure 9 extended to up to 20 populations × 200 generations, with N from 10 to 5000 and toggles for drift, selection (viability fitness for each genotype), mutation (A→B and B→A) and migration (fraction m from a source with frequency pm). With drift off, the model uses deterministic recursions. A dashed line always shows the infinite-population expectation. Population 1's genotype counts are tabulated with a chi-square each generation.
    • Design: an experiment runner that varies one parameter (N, p₀, w BB, w AB, μ, m) across student-chosen levels with replicate populations, graphing final p, heterozygosity or % loss/fixation with ±2 SE bars. This plays the role of the manual's option 2 (moving to more sophisticated prebuilt models).

    Limits: one locus, two alleles, discrete non-overlapping generations, random mating only (no inbreeding or assortative mating), viability selection only, constant zygote number N, migrants added after selection. Students do not build the spreadsheet themselves on this page; consider requiring a rebuild in Google Sheets for full credit, as the manual stresses that the model-building process is where the learning happens.

    Common misconceptions

    Watch for these ideas

    • "Dominant alleles become more common." Dominance has no effect on allele frequency in the null model; polydactyly is a rare dominant trait.
    • "Hardy-Weinberg is what populations usually do." It is a null model, a baseline that real populations are compared against. Its usefulness comes from detecting departures.
    • "p should be 0.5 in equilibrium." Any p can be at equilibrium; the model predicts that p stays where it starts.
    • "Small samples that deviate prove evolution by selection." In small populations drift alone causes large changes. Students should separate random (drift) from directional (selection) change by using replicates and the infinite-N expectation.
    • "Selection removes recessive alleles quickly." When q is small, most copies are hidden in heterozygotes, so even lethal recessive alleles decline very slowly (qt = q₀ / (1 + t·q₀)).
    • "If allele frequencies did not change, genotypes must be in HW proportions" (and vice versa). Selection can produce adult genotypes that deviate from p², 2pq, q² even though zygotes start in HW proportions.
    Suggested answers

    Pre-lab and expected results

    Pre-lab

    1. 2pq = 0.48.
    2. q = √(1/2500) = 0.02.
    3. 2pq ≈ 0.039 (about 1 in 26 are carriers).
    4. Most copies are in heterozygous carriers, hidden from selection.
    5. Dominance describes the heterozygote phenotype, not allele frequency.
    6. Shows A with probability 0.7, B with probability 0.3.
    7. Large population, random mating, no selection, no mutation, no migration.
    8. Genetic drift.

    Card simulation

    Case I: p wanders randomly around 0.5 (often by ±0.1–0.2 in 5 generations with 24 students). Case II: p rises steadily as B is removed, but B persists in heterozygotes. Case III: p tends toward an intermediate equilibrium (with these fitnesses, wAA = 0.5, wAB = 1, wBB = 0, the stable value is p̂ = 2/3), with noise. Case IV: isolated groups diverge strongly; some fix or lose A within a few generations while the class average changes less.

    Spreadsheet model

    • With 16 zygotes, Δp in one generation is often ±0.05–0.15; with 1000 zygotes it is usually within ±0.02. The expected SD of p after one generation is √(pq/2N).
    • Chi-square for HW proportions usually falls below 3.84 (about 95% of runs), as expected when the null is true.
    • I5 in English: "If the zygote is AB, write 1; otherwise, if it is BA, write 1; otherwise write 0." Both orders are heterozygotes because either gamete can carry A.
    • No randomness: genotypes would be exactly p²N, 2pqN, q²N and p would not change.

    Multi-generation model (deterministic expectations, p₀ = 0.5, 50 generations)

    • Drift only: expected p stays 0.5. With N = 10 most populations fix or lose A within 50 generations; with N = 100 final p spreads widely (SD ≈ 0.2–0.3) with occasional fixation; with N = 1000 p stays near 0.5 (SD ≈ 0.08).
    • Selection against BB, wBB = 0.8: p → about 0.90. wBB = 0.5: p ≈ 0.96. wBB = 0 (recessive lethal): p ≈ 0.98, q ≈ 0.019 (= 0.5 / (1 + 50 × 0.5)).
    • Heterozygote advantage (w = 0.8 / 1 / 0.8): p stays at 0.5, a stable polymorphism; from other starting values it moves toward 0.5.
    • Mutation μ = 0.01 (A→B) only: p falls to about 0.30 after 50 generations (pt = p₀(1 − μ)t).
    • Migration m = 0.05 from pm = 0.9: p rises to about 0.87, approaching pm.
    • With selection on, adults deviate from HW proportions (a deficit of BB); zygotes, formed before selection, are always in HW proportions.

    Analysis questions

    • In an infinite population with no evolutionary forces, allele frequencies do not change. In a small population with random gamete sampling, p changes unpredictably from generation to generation (drift); the direction cannot be predicted, but the average amount of change can, and it is larger in smaller populations.
    • Factors that change allele frequencies: selection, mutation, migration (gene flow), genetic drift; nonrandom mating changes genotype frequencies.
    • The CF allele persists because most copies are in carriers; possible heterozygote advantage and mutation also contribute. Polydactyly is rare because its allele has a low p, regardless of dominance.
    Student-designed investigations

    Questions that work well with the model

    • How small must N be before more than half of populations lose or fix an allele in 50 generations?
    • How does starting p affect the probability that A is fixed by drift? (Expect probability ≈ p₀.)
    • Compare selection against a recessive (wBB < 1) with selection against a dominant phenotype (wAA = wAB < 1).
    • How much migration overcomes selection, or prevents drift from fixing alleles (roughly one migrant per generation)?
    • Mutation-selection balance: with a recessive lethal and mutation μ, where does q settle (q ≈ √μ)?
    • Heterozygote advantage: does the equilibrium depend on starting p? How does drift interact with it in small populations?
    Extensions and assessment

    Going further

    • Supertaster/PTC lab (McMahon 2008, ABLE Proceedings): the intermediate taster phenotype allows genotype counts and a real chi-square test of Hardy-Weinberg in the class.
    • Rebuild the model in Google Sheets or Excel (COUNTIF is a simpler alternative to the nested IF), add a second generation by seeding it with the new p, and chart p over generations.
    • Explore Deme, AlleleA1 or the Radford simulation to test the questions above with more parameters.
    • Summative assessment: miniposters that state a hypothesis, show model runs as evidence, and receive peer review before teacher evaluation.
    • Reading: Cohen (2004), "Mathematics is biology's next microscope, only better"; Otto and Day (2007) for the modeling steps.
