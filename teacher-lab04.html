Diffusion and Osmosis

What causes my plants to wilt if I forget to water them?

Unit 2: Cell Structure and FunctionENE-1.BENE-1.CENE-2.HScience Practices 4.A, 5.A≈ 4 class periods
    At a glance

    Timing, alignment, and flow

    • Timing: at least four 45-minute periods. Plan one each for Procedures 1, 2, and 3, plus one for discussion and student designs. The pre-lab and Background can be homework. The potato-core design needs an overnight soak (set up on day 1, weigh on day 2). Cores left much longer than a day turn mushy.
    • Enduring understandings: ENE-2 (membranes let cells keep internal environments different from the outside), ENE-1 (living systems need constant energy input and exchange of materials).
    • Learning objectives: ENE-1.B (SA:V and exchange), ENE-1.C (specialized exchange structures), ENE-2.H (concentration gradients and movement across membranes).
    • Science practices: 5.A mathematical calculations (SA, V, % change, ψS = −iCRT); 4.A constructing graphs (% change vs. molarity with a fitted line).
    • Student page flow: Overview → Background → 8-item pre-lab quiz → Procedure 1 (agar cubes) → Procedure 2 (dialysis bags) → Procedure 3A (plasmolysis) → Procedure 3B (cores in color-coded sucrose plus a ψ calculator) → Design → Analyze (CER) → Evaluate → Submit (copy all responses).
    Preparation

    Real materials (summary)

    Procedure 1 · Agar cells

    • 2% agar with phenolphthalein: dissolve 1 g phenolphthalein in 100 mL 95% ethanol (1%). Heat 20 g agar in 1 L water until clear, cool to ~55 °C, add 10 mL of the 1% indicator, then add dilute NaOH until the agar is bright pink. Pour 3 cm deep and let it set overnight. Faded agar can be re-pinked by soaking it in dilute NaOH.
    • 0.1 M NaOH (0.4 g per 100 mL, plastic bottle) and 0.1 M HCl (0.83 mL concentrated 12.1 M HCl per 100 mL). Label both as hazardous.
    • Alternative: 1 packet unflavored gelatin in 237 mL water + 2.5 mL 1% phenolphthalein + a few drops 0.1 M NaOH. White vinegar can replace the HCl.
    • Plastic knives or plastic squares, metric rulers, Petri dishes, spill-proof trays.

    Procedure 2 · Dialysis model cells

    • 1 M sucrose (342.3 g/L), 1 M NaCl (58.4 g/L), 1 M glucose (180.2 g/L), 5% ovalbumin (50 g/L ≈ 0.0011 M).
    • Dialysis tubing cut into 20 cm pieces and soaked in water (store extra in 20% ethanol in the refrigerator), cups, and balances. Each bag holds 10 mL, with room left for water to enter.

    Procedure 3 · Living cells

    • Elodea tips (restricted in some states) or Mnium hornum moss, red onion as a substitute, slides, cover slips, and microscopes at 400×.
    • Potatoes, sweet potatoes, or yams; cork borers or a french-fry cutter; balances; cups.
    • 2,000 mL of 1.0 M sucrose (684.6 g). Dilute to 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, and 0.2 M (800, 600, 400, or 200 mL stock made up to 1 L). Dye each solution a different color and do not label the concentrations.
    Safety

    HCl and NaOH cause chemical burns. Require goggles, gloves, and aprons, prepare the solutions in a hood, and use trays.

    Simulation ↔ bench

    How the simulations map to the procedure

    • Indicator test (Step 1): two tubes. HCl leaves phenolphthalein colorless; NaOH turns it pink.
    • Agar cubes: students choose three shapes (0.5–3 cm cubes, a rod, a column, a slab, a plate), the acid (0.1 M HCl or vinegar), and the soak time (2–20 min). The animation shows mid-block cross-sections with the pink core shrinking. Depth follows a reaction–diffusion front, d = k√t (≈4.5 mm in 10 min for 0.1 M HCl), with about 6% block-to-block noise, read to the nearest 0.5 mm. Students type SA, V, SA:V, and % volume diffused, and a checker grades each row.
    • Dialysis bags: five bags, each with a selectable inside and cup solution, run for 30 minutes and weighed on a 0.01 g balance with blotting noise. Water flux follows Lp·RTΣσii(Δc), and solutes leak with first-order permeabilities (NaCl fastest, then glucose, then sucrose; ovalbumin cannot pass). Students compute % change, and the checker grades it. The bar chart shows means ± 2 SE.
    • Plasmolysis: an Elodea or red onion field at 400× in water, 0.2–1 M sucrose, 1 M glucose, 1 M NaCl, or 5% ovalbumin, followed by a distilled-water rinse. Each cell has its own solute potential (Elodea ≈ −8.6 bars, onion ≈ −11 bars, SD 12%), so intermediate solutions plasmolyze only some cells. Students count the plasmolyzed cells themselves (the incipient-plasmolysis method).
    • Potato cores: the six colors are randomly mapped to 0–1.0 M once per browser. Students identify them by testing water-filled dialysis bags (or reveal the labels if you allow it), then soak cores of white potato, sweet potato, yam, or potato left dry for 3 days. Isotonic points in the model are ≈0.30, 0.50, 0.40, and 0.42 M. Students read the x-intercept from the fitted line and calculate ψ, and the page checks the result against their own data.
    • Design: cube side length, shape at equal volume, acid temperature, HCl concentration, or sucrose concentration inside a bag, with 1–6 replicates. Output is a summary table (mean, SD, SE, ±2 SE), a graph with error bars, and slope/R² for numeric variables.
    Limits to discuss

    The agar model ignores the block face resting on the dish and treats the color front as perfectly sharp. The dialysis model is ideal: no tubing leaks, no hydrostatic pressure from a full bag, and ideal i values (real 1 M NaCl has i ≈ 1.8). Plasmolysis is simplified to 2-D protoplast area, and the solutes are treated as fully impermeant. Potato cores have no cut-surface damage or starch differences. Real data are noisier, so the simulation is a rehearsal and a data source, not a replacement for wet work.

    Misconceptions

    What students commonly get wrong

    • "Molecules stop moving at equilibrium." Equilibrium is dynamic: movement continues, with equal rates in both directions. The background diagram and analysis question 3 target this.
    • Amount vs. concentration. A cell in isotonic saline does not change no matter how much saline surrounds it.
    • Hypertonic/hypotonic as absolute labels. These terms compare two solutions and describe solute concentration, not water concentration.
    • Diffusion is directional. Diffusion comes from random motion. A drop of coins spreads out in every direction, and dropping them from higher (more energy) spreads them farther.
    • Bigger molecules diffuse just as fast. The dump-truck vs. small-car analogy helps: higher molecular weight means slower diffusion.
    • Small blocks have a faster diffusion rate. In Procedure 1, the front speed (mm/min) is about the same for every block. What differs is the fraction of the volume reached. Students often misstate this.
    • Water potential sign confusion. Solutes make ψ more negative. Water moves toward the more negative ψ.
    Answer key

    Suggested answers and expected results

    Pre-lab

    1. Kinetic energy is energy of motion; potential energy is stored (position, bonds, gradients).
    2. Higher temperature, steeper gradient, smaller solute. A larger cell and a thicker barrier slow diffusion.
    3. SA:V of a 2 cm cube = 24/8 = 3 cm⁻¹.
    4. (8 − 1)/8 = 87.5%.
    5. ψS = −(2)(0.1)(0.0831)(298) = −4.95 bars.
    6. Cell ψS = −7.43 bars < −4.95, so water moves into the cell.
    7. ψP = −4.95 − (−7.43) = +2.48 bars.
    8. Saltwater lowers soil ψ below root ψ, water leaves the roots, and the plant wilts or its cells plasmolyze.

    Procedure 1

    Expect a front of ≈4.5 mm after 10 min in 0.1 M HCl. The 1 cm cube is ≈100% clear, the 2 cm cube ≈90%, and the 3 cm cube ≈70%. Thin slabs, plates, and rods (high SA:V) clear faster than a cube of similar volume. % volume diffused rises with SA:V while front speed stays constant, which is why cells are small or elongated, flattened, or folded.

    Procedure 2

    • Typical 30-minute changes: 1 M sucrose in water ≈ +15%, glucose ≈ +10%, NaCl ≈ +8%, ovalbumin in water ≈ 0%, water in water ≈ 0%. Reversing a pair (water inside, sucrose outside) gives about −13%.
    • NaCl should be the most hypertonic by −iCRT (i = 2), but it leaks through the tubing quickly, which collapses the gradient and carries mass out. So in the tubing it appears less hypertonic than sucrose. This is a great discussion point about selective permeability.
    • 5% ovalbumin is only ≈0.0011 M (45,000 g/mol), so it produces almost no osmotic gradient even though it is "5%". Test glucose leakage with glucose test strips or Benedict's reagent on the cup water.
    • To find the solute concentration inside a living cell, bathe cells or tissue in a concentration series and find the solution that causes no net change (zero mass change, or 50% incipient plasmolysis).

    Procedure 3

    • Elodea in 1 M NaCl or 1 M sucrose plasmolyzes within seconds to a minute: chloroplasts crowd into a shrunken protoplast pulled away from the wall. Distilled water and 5% ovalbumin show no plasmolysis (cells stay turgid). Partial plasmolysis around 0.4 M sucrose for Elodea and 0.4–0.6 M for onion. Rinsing with water reverses it (deplasmolysis).
    • White potato: fitted x-intercept ≈ 0.30–0.35 M, so ψ = −(1)(0.33)(0.0831)(295) ≈ −8 bars. Sweet potato ≈ 0.5 M (≈ −12 bars), yam ≈ 0.4 M, and dried potato ≈ 0.42 M. Water loss concentrates solutes and lowers ψ. Fitted lines overestimate the isotonic point slightly because the curve flattens at high concentrations, which is worth discussing.
    • Potatoes in wet soil take up only a little water: once turgor balances the solute potential, net flow stops, and the skin (periderm) limits uptake.

    Analyzing results

    1. Small size and convoluted membranes keep SA:V high so exchange can keep up with metabolic demand.
    2. Mitochondria (cristae), chloroplasts (thylakoids), the ER, and the Golgi have folded membranes, which add surface area for membrane-bound enzymes and transport.
    3. Yes. Water crosses in both directions at equal rates, so there is no net osmosis.

    CER (example): Without water, soil ψ falls below the ψ of root and leaf cells. Water leaves the cells, turgor pressure falls toward zero, and the protoplasts may plasmolyze, so the stems lose support and wilt. Evidence: mass loss of cores in solutions with ψ below the tissue's, and plasmolysis in hypertonic solutions.

    Inquiry

    Ideas for student-designed investigations

    • Shape at constant volume (cube vs. rod vs. plate) and % diffused, linked to root hairs and microvilli.
    • Temperature of the acid bath and diffusion depth, linked to kinetic energy.
    • Acid concentration and depth: does doubling the concentration double the depth? (In the model, depth ∝ √C.)
    • Five pairs of solutions with a water–water control. Sucrose concentration inside the bag vs. % mass change gives a linear dose–response.
    • Compare ψ of different tubers or of potato stored under different conditions. Combine with Procedure 1 by cutting cores of different sizes.
    • Celery sticks soaked in water vs. 1 M NaCl: how far can they bend before snapping? Have students explain the "snap".
    Extensions

    Going further

    • Fungal turgor: soak mushroom slices in a sucrose series. A shift in mass or texture suggests their walls hold turgor too.
    • Food preservation: jams and salted meats have such low ψ that microbes lose water and cannot grow.
    • IV fluids: 0.9% saline (≈0.15 M NaCl) is isotonic to blood. Pure water would lyse red blood cells.
    • Transpiration link: revisit ψP as negative tension in xylem in the transpiration investigation.
    • Optional online tutorials: Molecular Workbench.
