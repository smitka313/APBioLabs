Photosynthesis

What factors affect the rate of photosynthesis in living leaves?

Unit 3: Cellular EnergeticsENE-1.IENE-1.JScience Practice 6.B≈ 3–4 class periods
    At a glance

    Timing, alignment, and flow

    • Timing: one 45-minute period to learn the floating disk technique and about two periods for student investigations. Add a third to share results as mini-posters, oral reports, or lab reports. Experimental design can be homework.
    • Enduring understanding: ENE-1. Objectives: ENE-1.I (photosynthetic processes that capture and store energy), ENE-1.J (capturing light energy and transferring it to molecules). Practice: 6.B (support a claim with evidence).
    • Skills reinforced: preparing solutions and dilutions, measuring light, building an index (ET50), using reciprocals, using medians, graphing, and communicating results.
    • Student page flow: Overview → Background (leaf anatomy, buoyancy, ET50, inverse square law) → 8-item pre-lab quiz → Sink disks (syringe vacuum) → Float assay (two cups, per-minute counts, ET50 check, dark option) → Design (6 independent variables, replicate cups, two graphs) → Analyze (CER) → Evaluate → Submit.
    Preparation

    Real materials (summary)

    • Baking soda (0.2% NaHCO3, 300 mL per experiment), dilute dish soap (≈5 mL in 250 mL water, one drop per cup, no suds).
    • Two or more 10 mL plastic syringes without needles (oral-medicine dispensers work). Test them first, because some fail under vacuum.
    • Smooth, thin leaves: baby spinach or English ivy. Avoid hairy leaves and big veins. Use a hole punch, clear plastic cups, and a timer.
    • A strong, cool light: an overhead projector bed, or a clamp light with a 100 W-equivalent CFL. A PAR meter helps a great deal, since foot-candles are not a good stand-in for photosynthetic photons.
    • Success depends on getting the disks to sink. Have every student practice the vacuum step. If disks will not sink after three tries, add soap rather than pulling more vacuums.
    Safety

    Solutions near electric lights: require goggles, keep water off hot bulbs (shatter hazard), and do not let students lean on lamps (burn hazard). Do not heat solutions above 50–60 °C for temperature studies.

    Simulation ↔ bench

    How the simulation maps to the procedure

    • Sink disks: two syringes of 10 disks each (bicarbonate and water). Each vacuum sinks each buoyant disk with a probability set by the amount of soap (no soap 12%, one drop 60%, more drops up to 80%). More than three vacuums damages disks: they rise slower and less evenly in the assay. Pouring with disks still afloat leaves them counted as floating at minute 0, a real student error.
    • Float assay: lamp at 15 cm (≈400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), spinach, 0.2% NaHCO3, 25 °C. The table records counts at each minute. The bicarbonate cup's ET50 is typically 9–12 min, matching the manual's sample (between 11 and 12 min). The water cup stays sunk. "Cover with a box" switches off photosynthesis: respiration uses up the stored O2 and disks re-sink more slowly than they rose (manual Figure 4). Students compute ET50 by interpolation and 1/ET50, and the page checks both against the recorded counts.
    • Rate model: gross = Pmax · I/(I+K) · C/(C+KC) · f(T) · age factor. Respiration has Q10 = 2, and net = gross − respiration. Light follows the inverse square law, with an action-spectrum weight per filter (green is weakest). f(T) has an optimum near 30 °C and denatures above ≈45 °C. Without a heat filter, a lamp very close to the cups warms the water. Each disk varies (lognormal rate, σ ≈ 0.25; ≈3% slow disks), so medians, SE, and replicate cups matter.
    • Design: lamp distance, filter color, bicarbonate %, temperature, plant type (spinach, sun or shade ivy, variegated ivy, pale lettuce, corn C4), or leaf age, with 1–5 cups per level. Output is a table of median ET50 and mean 1/ET50 ± 2 SE, plus side-by-side ET50 and 1/ET50 graphs that show the inverse vs. direct relationship (manual Figures 8a/8b).
    Limits to discuss

    The simulation treats buoyancy as a simple O2 threshold. It ignores solution depth, disks sticking to cup walls, CO2 depletion over time, and photoinhibition. Real filters vary widely in transmission. The plant parameters are plausible but not measured values, so treat comparisons between species as hypotheses to test at the bench.

    Misconceptions

    What students commonly get wrong

    • "Plants photosynthesize; animals respire." Plant cells have mitochondria and respire all the time. The dark experiment makes this visible.
    • Floating = gross photosynthesis. The disks measure net O2 accumulation, gross minus respiration.
    • Bigger ET50 means more photosynthesis. It is the reverse. That is why students plot 1/ET50.
    • Light intensity falls linearly with distance. It follows 1/d². Doubling the distance gives ¼ the light. A quick flashlight-on-graph-paper demo helps.
    • Green light is best because plants are green. Green is reflected or transmitted, so it is absorbed least.
    • The bicarbonate is "food". It is the inorganic carbon source (CO2) for the Calvin cycle, not an energy source.
    Answer key

    Suggested answers and expected results

    Pre-lab

    1. 6 O2 per glucose (1 O2 per CO2 fixed).
    2. Vacuum removes gas from the spongy mesophyll, solution fills the spaces, and the denser disk sinks.
    3. They float sooner, so ET50 decreases.
    4. 10 + (5 − 4)/(6 − 4) = 10.5 min.
    5. 1/8 = 0.125 min⁻¹.
    6. (10/20)² = 0.25.
    7. Chlorophylls absorb red and blue strongly and green weakly.
    8. The median resists late or non-rising disks (outliers).

    Expected simulation results

    • Light distance (with heat filter): 1/ET50 rises steeply at low light and levels off above ≈400–800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, a saturating light response curve. At 45 cm, disks rarely float within 45 minutes because respiration nearly matches photosynthesis there (close to the light compensation point). Without the heat filter, 5–8 cm heats the water and the rate falls, as with the data point the manual excluded.
    • Color: white > red ≈ blue > orange/violet > yellow > green. Through a green filter, disks may never reach 50%.
    • Bicarbonate: no floating at 0%, and the rate rises and saturates by ≈0.2–0.4% (CO2 limitation, then light limitation).
    • Temperature: slow at 10 °C, fastest around 25–32 °C, slower by 40 °C, and none at 50 °C (enzyme denaturation, rising respiration).
    • Plant type: at 15 cm, spinach ≈ corn ≥ sun ivy ≈ shade ivy > pale lettuce and variegated ivy. Corn pulls ahead in bright light and low CO2. Shade ivy saturates at lower light.
    • Leaf age: mature > young (immature chloroplasts, higher respiration) > old/yellowing (chlorophyll breakdown).

    Procedure prompts

    • The water-only cup is the control. Without a CO2 source the Calvin cycle stalls, net O2 output is near zero or negative, and disks stay down. This shows that the floating depends on carbon fixation.
    • In the dark, disks sink as respiration uses up stored O2. The sinking slope is shallower because respiration is slower than light-saturated photosynthesis, and the disks hold extra O2 beyond what they need to float.
    • Gross ≈ net (from floating) + respiration (from the rate of sinking in the dark).

    Analysis

    The ET50 graph slopes down while 1/ET50 slopes up, because rate ∝ 1/time. Variability is shown with ±2 SE error bars on mean 1/ET50 across cups, or with box plots. The light reactions produce O2 (from splitting water), ATP, and NADPH; the Calvin cycle needs CO2. Light and color act mainly on the light reactions, bicarbonate and temperature mainly on the Calvin cycle and rubisco. A disk can photosynthesize and still not float if gross ≤ respiration.

    CER (light example): Claim: net photosynthesis increases with light intensity and then levels off. Evidence: mean 1/ET50 rose from about 0.03 to 0.14 min⁻¹ between 45 and 10 cm, with smaller gains at high light and non-overlapping error bars at low light. Reasoning: more photons drive more electron transport and O2 release until another factor (CO2 supply, rubisco capacity) limits the rate.

    Inquiry

    Ideas for student-designed investigations

    • Light response curves for sun vs. shade leaves (a two-variable design).
    • Action spectrum with colored filters, ideally controlling for photon flux with a PAR meter.
    • CO2 limitation: a bicarbonate dilution series.
    • Temperature with water baths, staying at or below 50–60 °C.
    • Respiration: float the disks, then measure the time to re-sink in the dark at different temperatures.
    • Method variables: disk size, solution depth, soap amount, reusing disks, storing infiltrated disks overnight.
    • Plant variables: variegated leaves, stomatal density, light-starved vs. light-grown plants, C3 vs. C4.
    Extensions

    Going further

    • Compare with O2 or CO2 gas sensors if probe systems are available.
    • Herbicide resistance assays with leaf disks (Hill and Steucek, 1985).
    • Peer-reviewed mini-posters with a shared rubric before final grading.
