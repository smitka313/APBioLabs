Biotechnology: Bacterial Transformation

How can we use genetic engineering techniques to manipulate heritable information?

Unit 6: Gene Expression and RegulationIST-1.PScience Practice 6.D
    At a glance

    Planning summary

    TimingDay 1: background and pre-lab (45–60 min, or homework). Day 2: transform and plate (45–60 min). Day 3: observe plates, count, calculate efficiency (45–60 min; plates may need more than 24 h). Day 4+: one or two periods for student-designed investigations and presentations.
    Unit / Big IdeasUnit 6 (Gene Expression and Regulation: biotechnology, operons). Big Idea 3 (Information), with a link to Big Idea 1 (selection on antibiotic resistance).
    Enduring understandingIST-1: Heritable information provides for continuity of life.
    ObjectiveIST-1.P: Explain the use of genetic engineering techniques in analyzing or manipulating DNA.
    Science practice6.D: Explain the relationship between experimental results and larger biological concepts, processes, or theories.
    Prior knowledgeDNA and RNA structure, transcription and translation, bacterial cell structure, membranes, the operon model, natural selection. Pipetting, metric volumes, scientific notation.
    SafetySterile technique; non-pathogenic E. coli K-12 strain; 10% bleach for surfaces and waste (20 min soak) or autoclave 20 min at 121 °C; ampicillin is a penicillin (students with penicillin allergy should avoid contact); goggles and gloves; UV lamps require UV-protective eyewear.
    Preparation

    Materials and setup

    Bench version (eight stations). E. coli (vial or slant), plasmid (pAMP or pGLO, about 20 µg), lyophilized ampicillin, 50 mM CaCl2 transformation solution (pH 6.1), LB agar powder (500 mL) and LB broth, sterile pipettes and loops, 60 mm Petri dishes, 2 mL microtubes, and (for pGLO) arabinose. Also needed: crushed ice, a 42 °C water bath with thermometer, a 37 °C incubator (or 48–72 h at room temperature), a timer for 50 s, bleach, and waste bags.

    • 3–7 days before: pour LB plates first, then add ampicillin (and arabinose for LB/amp/ara) once the agar cools to about 50 °C (you can hold the flask). Ampicillin is destroyed at ≥ 60 °C; agar sets at 27 °C. About 12 mL per plate. Cure 2 days at room temperature, then store inverted at 4 °C for up to 30 days.
    • 24–36 h before: rehydrate E. coli with 250 µL transformation solution and streak starter plates for single colonies (four quadrants). Incubate at 37 °C. Do not refrigerate starter plates: cells must be actively growing. Rehydrate the plasmid and use it within 24 h.
    • Just before class: aliquot 1 mL transformation solution (kept on ice) and 1 mL LB broth per station.

    The manual's protocol uses two LB and two LB/amp plates per group with pAMP. This page uses the pGLO version, with LB, LB/amp, and LB/amp/ara plates for each tube (six plates; 3 × 100 µL is drawn from each 510 µL tube). If you run pGLO at the bench with four plates, drop the −pGLO LB/amp/ara and +pGLO LB plates.

    How the simulation maps to the real procedure

    What is real, what is modeled

    • Protocol stepper: the ten steps follow the student manual (Steps 2–17): 250 µL cold CaCl2, ice, a colony into each tube, 10 µL plasmid (0.005 µg/µL, the manual's value) into +pGLO only, 10 min on ice, 42 °C for 50 s, 2 min on ice, 250 µL LB and 10 min recovery, then 100 µL per plate and 24 h at 37 °C. Students can change the solution, amount of cells, plasmid volume, ice time, bath temperature and time, and recovery time, or skip the ice, heat-shock, and return-to-ice steps.
    • Outcome model: expected transformant colonies on one plate = 30 × a product of factors for each step, with lognormal batch-to-batch noise (SD 0.3) and Poisson counting. With the manual's settings this gives about 30 colonies, or ≈ 3 × 103 transformants/µg, inside the 8.0 × 102 to 7.0 × 103 range the manual quotes. The factors follow the manual's statements: no heat shock gives about 10-fold fewer transformants; 90 s gives about half as many as 50 s; more plasmid saturates uptake so efficiency per µg falls; skipping CaCl2 nearly abolishes transformation; skipping recovery lowers survival on ampicillin. Very hot or long shocks kill cells, which also thins the lawn on LB plates.
    • Plates: −pGLO grows as a lawn on LB and not at all on ampicillin plates. +pGLO gives a lawn on LB (no glow, no arabinose), white colonies on LB/amp, and colonies that glow under UV on LB/amp/ara. At 48 h, small non-glowing satellite colonies appear around transformants.
    • Counting and calculation: students mark colonies on an enlarged plate, then enter DNA mass, fraction spread, DNA spread, efficiency, and scientific notation. The page checks each value against that run's actual volumes.
    • Limits: no contamination, no sterile-technique errors, no growth-phase effects of old starter plates, and no mutagenesis. Satellites are untransformed in 95% of streak tests. The numbers are a model, not data; real classroom efficiencies vary more.
    Common misconceptions

    Watch for these

    • "Ampicillin causes the bacteria to become resistant." Ampicillin selects; it does not induce the bla gene into existence. Resistance came from the plasmid.
    • "Transformed cells glow on every plate." GFP is under the arabinose promoter. Without arabinose, AraC keeps it off, so +pGLO colonies on LB/amp are white.
    • "No growth on −pGLO LB/amp means the cells were dead." The −pGLO LB plate (a lawn) shows they were alive; they were killed by ampicillin.
    • "More DNA is always better." Uptake saturates. Extra plasmid raises colonies only slightly and lowers efficiency per µg.
    • "Every colony on the LB/amp plate is a transformant." Satellites around large colonies usually are not; they survive because secreted beta-lactamase destroys nearby ampicillin.
    • Fraction errors. Students often use 100/250 or 100/260 instead of 100/510 because they forget the LB broth added in Step 13.
    • Efficiency vs. percent transformed. Efficiency is per microgram of DNA, not the fraction of cells transformed (only about 1% of cells are transformed under lab conditions).

    Efficiency calculation (manual values)

    DNA used = 0.005 µg/µL × 10 µL = 0.05 µg. Total volume = 250 + 10 + 250 = 510 µL. Fraction spread = 100/510 = 0.196. DNA spread = 0.05 × 0.196 = 0.0098 µg. With 30 colonies, efficiency = 30/0.0098 ≈ 3.1 × 103 transformants/µg. 10,000 = 1 × 104; 5,000 = 5 × 103.

    Pre-lab quiz

    Answers and explanations appear on the student page when students check each item: 1 white, amp-sensitive; 2 no (selection acts on existing variation); 3 −pGLO LB/amp and +pGLO LB/amp; 4 Ca2+ neutralizes charge repulsion; 5 white, no glow without arabinose; 6 0.05 µg; 7 0.196; 8 ≈ 4.1 × 103/µg.

    Analyzing results

    • Before 1: −pGLO LB (and +pGLO LB): no selection, so untransformed cells dominate.
    • Before 2: +pGLO LB/amp and LB/amp/ara: only cells carrying bla survive ampicillin.
    • Before 3: −pGLO LB/amp vs. +pGLO LB/amp (plasmid is the only variable); LB plates confirm viability; LB/amp vs. LB/amp/ara shows GFP induction.
    • Before 4: negatively charged DNA and membrane repel; the membrane is a hydrophobic barrier; plasmids are large; cells must be competent (late log phase); restriction enzymes may destroy foreign DNA.
    • Before 5: Ca2+ bridges DNA to the lipopolysaccharide; cold rigidifies the membrane; the rapid 0 → 42 → 0 °C shift creates a thermal imbalance and transient pores that let plasmids in.
    • After 1–3: +pGLO cells gained ampicillin resistance and arabinose-inducible fluorescence; colony shape and off-white color (without arabinose) did not change. The −pGLO controls received identical treatment without plasmid and did not grow on ampicillin.
    • After 4: saves energy and resources; GFP (or the native ara genes) is made only when the sugar is present to use.
    • After 5 / efficiency: compare to 8.0 × 102–7.0 × 103. Lower values: no or wrong heat shock, warm CaCl2, skipped recovery, too few or too many cells, old starter plate, plasmid not delivered. Higher values: very competent cells, counting satellites, or longer recovery allowing cell division before plating.

    Evaluating results

    Question 5: a cell can take in a plasmid with genes it already has, but you could not detect it by selection, so efficiency would be underestimated (or overestimated if the host already had resistance). Satellite streak test: large colonies grow on LB/amp/ara and glow; satellites grow only on LB and do not glow, so they lack pGLO and should not be counted.

    Ideas for student-designed investigations

    What students can test

    • Built into the page: heat-shock time (expect a peak near 50 s), heat-shock temperature (peak near 42 °C, sharp drop above 45 °C), plasmid volume (efficiency per µg falls as volume rises), time on ice, recovery time, amount of cells, and the transformation solution. Encourage 3+ replicates and 2 SE error bars.
    • Satellite colonies: streak satellites and large colonies onto LB, LB/amp, and LB/amp/ara (manual's suggested design).
    • Bench extensions from the manual: growth stage of bacteria; ratio of transformation solution; safe mutagens (dilute H2O2, caffeine, UV with goggles, potassium nitrate) and their effect on plasmid expression; uptake of two plasmids; competition between transformed and untransformed cells on one plate.
    • Remind students to keep a −plasmid control and LB plates in every design.
    Extensions

    Where students can go from here

    • Research Griffith's 1928 experiment and the "transforming principle" (later Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty); present as an annotated diagram.
    • Draw the arabinose operon with and without arabinose and compare it to the lac operon.
    • Debate GM foods (e.g., Bt corn) and respond to "Just because science can do something doesn't mean that it should."
    • Isolate plasmid DNA from transformants and confirm it by restriction digest and gel electrophoresis (connects to Investigation 9).
    • If transformation fails at the bench, the manual suggests generating random colony counts so students can still practice the efficiency calculation; this page can serve that purpose.
