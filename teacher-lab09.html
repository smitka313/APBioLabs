Restriction Enzyme Analysis of DNA

How can we use genetic information to identify and profile individuals?

Unit 6: Gene Expression and RegulationIST-1.PScience Practice 6.D
    At a glance

    Planning summary

    TimingDay before: about 15 min to make buffer and agarose, plus 30 min to set up stations (20 min more if you cast the gels). Day 1: 45 min for the scenario, background and pre-lab (can be homework), 20 min for pipetting practice and loading, then 45 min to 2 h of electrophoresis, 15–30 min staining and 45 min to overnight destaining. Day 2: 45–60 min for analysis and the "whodunit" write-up. The simulation alone takes about two periods.
    Unit / Big IdeaBig Idea 3 (storage, retrieval and transmission of genetic information), Unit 6 (biotechnology). Connects to evolution through gene sequencing and genetic variation.
    Enduring understandingIST-1: Heritable information provides for continuity of life.
    ObjectiveIST-1.P Explain the use of genetic engineering techniques in analyzing or manipulating DNA.
    Science practice6.D Explain the relationship between experimental results and larger biological concepts, processes, or theories.
    SkillsPipetting and gel loading, running electrophoresis, plotting on semi-log paper, building a standard curve and interpolating from it.
    Preparation

    Real materials (summary)

    • Kit (8 stations): 20-µL vials of uncut λ DNA, λ/EcoRI and λ/HindIII (optional: λ cut with other enzymes for evidence samples), agarose, 50× TAE, methylene blue gel/buffer stain, staining trays, transfer pipettes, gloves, semi-log paper and rulers.
    • Not supplied: gel boxes and power supplies, a 55–65 °C water bath, a microwave or hot plate, 1-L graduated cylinder, a 5-L container, distilled water, masking tape, markers, foil, and racks.
    • 1× TAE: 100 mL of 50× with 4,900 mL of water (8 stations), or 50 mL with 2,450 mL (4 stations).
    • 0.8% agarose: 5 g in 625 mL of 1× TAE (or 2.4 g in 300 mL). Melt fully, hold at 55–65 °C, and pour about 50 mL per tray, about halfway up the comb teeth.
    • Methylene blue in gel/buffer: at ≤50 V use 400 µL per 300 mL of agarose and 500 µL per 500 mL of buffer. At ≥50 V use 480 µL and 960 µL. Final stain: undiluted, no longer than 1 h, then destain in distilled water (chlorine fades bands).
    • Evidence samples: relabel vials as "Blood," "Ms. Mason," "Mr. Gladson," "Bobby" and so on, using λ cut with different enzymes or mixtures. Keep a HindIII vial as the marker. Pool the DNA by a quick spin or by tapping the tubes.
    • Practice gels: old agarose or triple-strength gelatin. Practice "samples": 10 drops of glycerol or corn syrup, 50 drops of water and 1 drop of food coloring.
    How the simulation maps to the bench

    Model and limits

    • Fragment sizes are real. They are computed from the published λ site coordinates (48,502 bp). EcoRI gives 21,226, 7,421, 5,804, 5,643, 4,878 and 3,530. HindIII gives 23,130, 9,416, 6,557, 4,361, 2,322, 2,027, 564 and 125. BamHI gives 16,841, 7,233, 6,770, 6,527, 5,626 and 5,505. Double digests are computed from the combined sites. The cos ends anneal in unheated samples (about 30%), so HindIII shows the 27,491 band (23,130 + 4,361) sitting on the 23,130 band, as the manual describes. Students can tick "Heat samples to 65 °C" to see this band disappear.
    • Migration: relative mobility is a logistic function of log10(bp). It is nearly linear from about 0.5 to 10 kb and compresses large fragments, so the 23 kb doublet and the largest BamHI and EcoRI fragments run "too fast" for the line. This is the real reason to leave them out of the fit. Agarose percentage shifts the resolving range. Voltage above 50 V speeds the run but reduces separation and widens bands. The dye front runs with about 300-bp DNA. Gel-to-gel speed varies by about ±3%, lanes "smile" slightly, and readings carry ±0.4 mm error, so repeat gels give a meaningful spread.
    • Visibility: band intensity scales with DNA mass (0.04 µg/µL × volume × fragment fraction). Methylene blue detects about 15 ng, so the 564- and 125-bp HindIII bands are invisible at normal loads, as the manual notes. Unstained gels show only faint bands, and measuring is allowed only after staining.
    • Procedure errors students can make: overfilling a well (more than 25 µL spills into neighboring lanes), reversed leads (DNA leaves the wells into the buffer), running too long (small fragments run off), running at high voltage (poor separation), and loading a gel that has already been run.
    • Limits: no casting or pipetting technique, no smearing from degraded DNA, and band shapes are idealized. The "crime" profiles are λ digests standing in for human RFLPs, just as in the real kit.
    Common misconceptions

    Watch for these

    • "Bigger fragments are heavier, so they sink faster." Movement is caused by the electric field, not gravity. Large fragments are slowed by the gel mesh.
    • "Each band is one molecule." A band holds millions of copies of the same-sized fragment, and co-migrating fragments of different sequence share one band.
    • Counting bands as fragments. Students should explain why the λ map predicts more fragments than they see (co-migration, faint small fragments, the cos doublet).
    • Plotting log(bp) on semi-log paper. On semi-log paper you plot the raw bp, because the axis takes the logarithm for you.
    • "A DNA match proves guilt." It shows only whose blood it is. Motive, means and opportunity are separate questions.
    • Thinking restriction enzymes cut at random. They cut only at specific recognition sequences, which is why the patterns are reproducible.
    Suggested answers

    Pre-lab, analysis and expected results

    Getting Started

    • Activity I: the complement is 3′-TTTCAGCGACCTTAAGTGACGTAGCTTAAGGGCCCCGATATATACCTTAAGCT-5′. EcoRI cuts at three GAATTC sites, giving 4 fragments (top strands 11, 14, 21 and 7 nt) with 5′-AATT overhangs. Any two EcoRI ends can be ligated. SmaI (one site, CCC↓GGG) gives 2 blunt fragments. There are no HindIII or BamHI sites.
    • Activity II: different people's DNA cut with the same enzyme gives different RFLP patterns only where their sequences differ at or between sites. Because more than 99% of DNA is shared, one enzyme often misses differences, so several enzymes or loci are used. Identical twins have identical patterns. Fraternal twins and triplets differ like ordinary siblings.
    • Activity III: DNA's phosphates are negative, so it moves toward the + (red) electrode. Wells go at the black end.
    • Quiz numbers: 4 fragments, 6 λ/EcoRI fragments, 10 mL of 50× TAE, about 2,820 bp.

    A typical standard curve fitted to the five resolved marker bands is log10(bp) ≈ 4.45 − 0.030·d (mm), with R² ≈ 0.99. Estimates are usually within 5–10% from 2 to 8 kb (for example, the 7,421 band reads about 7,400–7,800). Fragments above about 10 kb are strongly underestimated (for example, 21,226 reads about 14,000–16,000) because they lie in the compressed region. Use this to discuss the limits of a standard curve.

    Analysis prompts

    • Log scale: sizes span more than two orders of magnitude, and distance is roughly linear in log(size).
    • Doublet: 23,130 and 27,491 do not resolve at 0.8% and sit off the line.
    • Visual analysis is not enough for court. Students should report sizes with an uncertainty (mean ± 2 SE from repeat gels or readings) and a stated match tolerance. The repeat-measurement spread (about 3–6%) suggests a tolerance of about 10%.
    • Sources of error: loading volume and overflow, uneven wells, voltage and heating, run time, ruler reading, measuring to the band center instead of the front edge, and co-migrating bands.

    Crime-scene case decoder

    Each case file number the student enters assigns each person a "profile" (a λ digest) and decides whose DNA is in the blood. Give different groups different numbers to vary the outcome, as the manual suggests. The default case is 102.

    There is no single "right" whodunit. The blood shows only whose blood it is. Mr. Gladson's email gives motive. Bobby could have cut himself on a flask he broke. Ms. Mason might have staged the scene as a forensics quiz. According to the manual, she was last seen relaxing poolside.

    Ideas for student-designed investigations

    Beyond the crime scene

    • How does agarose percentage (0.6–1.2%) change the resolving range and the accuracy of size estimates for small versus large fragments?
    • Is there a trade-off between voltage and separation? Compare estimate error at 50, 100 and 150 V.
    • How many repeat gels are needed before the ± 2 SE of an estimate falls below 5%?
    • Does heating the samples remove the cos-end bands, and does that change which marker points belong in the fit?
    • Groups write a mock case for other groups (the manual's alternative), choosing which digest is the culprit.
    Extensions

    Going further

    • Stage a mock trial with a civics class. The defense argues the data are unreliable, and the prosecution presents them clearly.
    • Discuss modern forensic STR/PCR profiling compared with RFLPs (Bio-Rad Forensic DNA Fingerprinting kit; "Get a Clue, Destiny").
    • Ethics: the Innocence Project, who owns genetic information, genetic testing and insurance, and benefit-sharing for crop genes. The film Gattaca works well for discussion.
    • Use NEB 1 kb and 100 bp ladders to show the log relationship directly.
