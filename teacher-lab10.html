Energy Dynamics

What factors govern energy capture, allocation, storage, and transfer between producers and consumers in a terrestrial ecosystem?

Unit 8: EcologyENE-1.M, ENE-1.N, ENE-1.O, SYI-3.AScience Practices 6.C, 6.D
    At a glance

    What students do and how long it takes

    Bench version2–3 weeks
    Class time (bench)2–3 × 1 h
    Simulation version2–3 periods
    Groupingteams of 3–4

    Students build a two-species model ecosystem (Wisconsin Fast Plants and cabbage white larvae) and keep an energy "budget." Part 1 estimates plant NPP from dry biomass at day 7 and day 14 and adds a dark-respiration measurement to estimate GPP. Part 2 measures consumption, growth, and frass for larvae feeding on a Brussels sprout and computes consumed, assimilated, egested, and respired energy. Part 3 is a student-designed investigation, presented as a mini-poster.

    • Learning objectives: ENE-1.M (strategies to acquire and use energy), ENE-1.N (effects of changes in energy availability), ENE-1.O (autotrophs and heterotrophs move energy through ecosystems), SYI-3.A (molecular variation and survival).
    • Science practices: 6.C (justify claims with evidence and theory), 6.D (connect results to larger biological concepts).
    • Placement: during ecology (Big Idea 4); also works with cellular energetics (Big Idea 2). Student questions often connect to natural selection (plant defense, food choice).
    Preparation

    Real materials (summary)

    • Fast Plant seeds (50–100), soil mix, wicking cord, bottle growing systems, fertilizer (10-10-10 soluble), a light bank or growth chamber, bee sticks for pollination.
    • About 20 cabbage white butterfly eggs, a Brassica Barn (deli-container) per team, paper-towel frass pads, Brussels sprouts (plus broccoli or cabbage for food-choice studies), honey water for adults.
    • Electronic balance reading to 0.001 g (or weigh groups of plants or larvae together and divide), aluminum-foil weigh boats, a drying oven or food dehydrator (about 100 °C for 48 h), digital cameras.
    • 10% bleach for cleaning chambers between generations.
    Start early

    Have students raise one generation of each organism before the experiment. It produces the extra plants and larvae the procedure needs and builds the familiarity that generates good questions.

    Safety and housekeeping
    • Pre-approve all proposals. Do not allow designs that stress live animals (for example, high temperatures). The simulation caps larval rearing temperature at 28 °C for this reason.
    • Pieris rapae is a USDA-listed pest: never release larvae or adults. Euthanize by freezing at the end.
    • Clean chambers with dilute bleach and dry them completely; keep water away from lights.
    How the simulation maps to the bench

    Models, calibration, and limits

    Part 1: Fast Plants

    • Mean dry mass per plant follows D(t) = 0.045 t1.147 g, calibrated to the manual's sample tables (0.42 g at day 7, 0.93 g at day 14). Each plant has its own lognormal "vigor," so harvests of 10 have realistic spread (SE ≈ 0.02–0.05 g).
    • Dry fraction rises with age (≈ 21.8% at day 7, 24.6% at day 14) matching the manual's 21.4% and 24.2%. Washing loses up to 4% of root mass; blotting adds ±0.02 g wet-mass noise.
    • Dark respiration is 4 mg CO2 per g dry mass per hour. With the conversions on the page (0.00364 mg per ppm in 2.0 L; 2.97 kcal per g CO2, i.e., 30/44 × 4.35), students get respiration ≈ 0.25–0.28 kcal/day for a 14-day plant and NPP/GPP ≈ 50–60%.
    • Limit: the manual's sample masses are large for real Fast Plants (real 14-day plants are far lighter). The simulation keeps the manual's numbers so results match the published tables; discuss the scale with students if they compare to real data. Respiration measured in the dark is assumed to equal respiration in the light.

    Part 2: Larvae

    • Per larva over 3 days: start ≈ 0.030 g, growth ≈ 6× (≈ 0.15 g gain), efficiency of conversion of ingested food ≈ 10% (dry basis), digestibility ≈ 75% → ≈ 1.6 g wet sprout eaten and ≈ 0.06 g dry frass. Group totals reproduce the manual's Table 3 (≈ 19 g sprout lost, 1.5 g gained, 0.5–0.6 g frass for 10 larvae).
    • A cut sprout loses ≈ 10% of its mass to evaporation in 3 days. The manual's 19 g "consumed" implicitly includes this water loss; the simulation adds a control sprout so students can correct it (≈ 16 g truly eaten).
    • The true larval dry fraction in the model is 0.16. The student manual tells students to assume 40%, while the teacher's sample table uses 0.15. Students can measure it ("freeze and dry 5 extra larvae") and see how the choice changes the respiration estimate. With 0.40 they get P/C ≈ 30%; with 0.16 they get P/C ≈ 12%, much closer to field values for caterpillars.
    • The plant ratio defaults to 0.15 (a measured Brussels sprout). If students use their Fast Plant ratio (≈ 0.22–0.25), consumed energy rises by about 50%: a good discussion of whether one plant's percent biomass can stand in for another's.

    Part 3: Design options

    • Plants: light (saturating response, half-saturation 150 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), temperature (Q10 ≈ 2 below 24 °C, heat stress above), crowding (per-plant biomass ∝ 1/(1 + 0.35(n − 1))), fertilizer (0, ½, 1, 2×; 2× mildly reduces growth). Each replicate is one plant harvested at day 14.
    • Larvae: food type (dry fraction and quality differ; lettuce, a non-brassica, is mostly refused because it lacks glucosinolate feeding cues), temperature (16–28 °C, Q10 ≈ 2), and density (food runs short above about 18 larvae per sprout). Each replicate is one barn.
    • The page reports mean, SD, SE, and ±2 SE intervals and flags pairs of levels whose intervals do not overlap. Remind students that overlap is a rough screen, not a formal test.

    What the simulation cannot do: it does not reproduce the daily care, life-cycle observations, mold, or disease that generate authentic questions on the bench, and it does not measure light energy input.

    Common misconceptions

    What to listen for

    • "Fresh weight is biomass." Most of a plant or sprout is water. Energy is in the dry organic matter.
    • "Energy is recycled like matter." Matter cycles; energy flows one way and leaves as heat at every transfer.
    • "Frass is respiration." Frass is egested (undigested) energy that goes to decomposers; respiration is energy released as heat and CO2.
    • "The 10% rule is a law." Transfer efficiency varies widely (roughly 5–20%) with organism, food quality, and temperature. Students' numbers will not be exactly 10%.
    • "NPP is how much light the plant absorbs." NPP is what remains after the plant's own respiration; GPP is the total captured.
    • "Sprout mass lost = sprout eaten." Evaporation also removes mass (but not energy).
    Suggested answers and expected results

    Answer key

    Pre-lab

    1. Most living mass is water, which holds no chemical energy and varies; dry mass is the biomass.
    2. 4.2 ÷ 19.6 × 100 = 21.4%.
    3. 4.2 × 4.35 = 18.27 kcal.
    4. 18.27 ÷ 10 ÷ 7 = 0.26 kcal per plant per day.
    5. Plants used part of the captured energy in their own respiration; the biomass weighed is what remained (NPP = GPP − R).
    6. 0.15 × 0.40 × 5.5 = 0.33 kcal.
    7. 0.05 × 4.76 = 0.238 kcal.
    8. Respiration, by difference: 1.24 − 0.33 − 0.24 ≈ 0.67 kcal.

    Part 2 typical results (10 larvae, ratios 0.15 plant / 0.40 larva)

    Sprout 26–33 g → 9–14 g (raw loss ≈ 17–20 g; corrected ≈ 14–17 g). Larvae ≈ 0.30 → 1.7–1.8 g. Frass ≈ 0.55–0.65 g. Per larva (corrected): C ≈ 0.95–1.1 kcal, F ≈ 0.28–0.31, P ≈ 0.30–0.33, R ≈ 0.35–0.45 kcal; A/C ≈ 70%, P/A ≈ 45%, P/C ≈ 30%. With the measured larval ratio (≈ 0.16), P drops to ≈ 0.13 kcal and R rises to ≈ 0.55 kcal (P/C ≈ 12%).

    Analysis prompts

    • Measured vs estimated: measured: plant dry mass, larval wet mass, sprout wet mass, frass dry mass, dark CO2. Calculated by difference: larval respiration. Assumed/estimated: energy per gram values, larval dry fraction, sprout dry fraction, light energy input, respiration in light, energy in uric acid within frass, heat from decomposition.
    • 10% rule: students' P/C values (≈ 12–30% depending on assumptions) exceed 10% because larvae are fast-growing juveniles on high-quality food and the larval dry fraction assumption inflates production. Whole-trophic-level efficiencies are lower because not all plant biomass is eaten and adults/other stages respire without growing.
    • CER exemplar: Claim: energy capture is limited by light and plant respiration, and most energy eaten by larvae is not stored. Evidence: NPP ≈ 0.3 kcal/day vs R ≈ 0.27 kcal/day; larvae stored ≈ 0.13–0.33 of ≈ 1.0 kcal eaten, egested ≈ 0.3 kcal. Reasoning: the second law—each transformation releases heat; respiration powers growth and activity; indigestible material (cellulose) is egested.

    Expected design trends

    • Light: biomass rises steeply from 50 to 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and levels off; % biomass slightly lower in dim light.
    • Temperature (plants): increases to ≈ 24 °C, declines sharply by 32 °C.
    • Crowding: per-plant biomass falls with plants per cell (≈ 1 : 0.74 : 0.59 : 0.49); total per cell rises.
    • Fertilizer: none ≈ 45% of standard; 2× slightly below standard.
    • Food: broccoli ≈ sprout > Fast Plant leaves > cabbage ≫ lettuce for growth; larvae eat more wet mass of watery foods.
    • Larval density: gain per larva drops once the sprout runs short (≈ 20–30 larvae).
    Ideas for student-designed investigations

    Questions that work well

    • Do all plants (or plant parts) have the same percent biomass? Compare Fast Plants, sprouts, broccoli, cabbage.
    • How much energy does it cost a larva to process different plant sources?
    • How does energy allocation to roots, shoots, and seeds change over the life cycle? How much goes to reproduction?
    • Limits to productivity: light, soil volume, CO2, crowding, leaf area.
    • Herbivory and plant response: early vs late herbivory, trichome defense, induced defenses and their cost.
    • Build a spreadsheet model of plant growth or larval growth from the Part 1 and 2 values and test its predictions.
    Extensions and assessment

    Going further

    • Mini-poster session: half the class presents while half reviews with a rubric, then switch. Students revise posters based on peer feedback before grading.
    • Bomb calorimetry discussion: where 4.35, 5.5, and 4.76 kcal/g come from, and why fats raise larval energy density.
    • Reading: Agren and Schemske (1993) on the cost of trichome defense in Brassica rapa; Williams and Hill (1986) introducing rapid-cycling Brassica.
    • Field connection: compare student efficiencies with published assimilation and production efficiencies for insects, endotherms, and ectotherms.
