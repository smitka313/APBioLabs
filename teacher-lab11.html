Transpiration

What factors, including environmental variables, affect the rate of transpiration in plants?

Unit 8: EcologyENE-3.DIST-5.ASYI-1.HScience Practices 3.C, 5.A≈ 4 × 45 min
    At a glance

    Timing, alignment, and flow

    • Timing: about four 45-minute periods: leaf area and stomatal peels; experimental design; at least one period running experiments; one period sharing class results. Potometer data are most reliable over longer runs (up to 24 h), and the whole-plant method needs daily weighings for several days.
    • Enduring understandings: ENE-3, IST-5, SYI-1. Learning objectives ENE-3.D, IST-5.A, SYI-1.H. The manual also places the lab under Big Idea 2 (cellular processes) and Big Idea 4 (interactions), with links to evolution.
    • Science practices: 3.C (procedures aligned to the question) and 5.A (mathematical calculations).
    • Prerequisites: cell structure and function, properties of water, osmosis, diffusion and active transport, water potential, photosynthesis gas exchange.
    • Page flow: Overview → Background → Pre-lab (8 auto-checked items + 2 diagram prompts) → Leaf area (grid tracing) → Stomata (nail-polish peel microscope) → Water loss (potometer / whole plant / pressure sensor; control vs. fan, lamp, mist, dark) → Design (seven variables, t-test) → Analyze → Evaluate → Submit.
    Preparation

    Real materials (summary)

    • Plants available locally: Impatiens (moisture-loving), Coleus, oleander (drought tolerant), bean seedlings, peas, tomato, peppers, ferns, or Wisconsin Fast Plants. Campus cuttings work for multi-species comparisons.
    • Microscopes, slides, clear cellophane tape, clear nail polish, scissors; graph paper (4 squares = 1 cm²) and metric rulers.
    • Potometers: clear plastic tubing in several diameters, ring stand and clamp, 0.1 mL or 1.0 mL pipettes, a needle-free syringe or wash bottle for filling, petroleum jelly or small clamps for sealing. Or a gas pressure sensor connected directly to the tubing.
    • Whole-plant method: small potted plants with many leaves, one-gallon plastic bags (no zipper), string, a balance.
    • Environmental treatments: fan, heat lamp, spray bottle and plastic bag, salt, other materials students request.
    Safety

    Razor blades and scalpels: cut away from fingers, ideally with the stem under water. Goggles during experiments. Nail polish is toxic by ingestion and inhalation; ventilate and avoid eye contact. Remove needles from syringes. Plant waste goes in the trash; tubing and pipettes can be reused.

    Bench tips from the manual
    • The water column must touch the cut end of the stem; a small gap stops uptake.
    • To fill tubing without bubbles, attach a syringe to the pipette end with rubber tubing and pull water up; leave it on to keep the water under tension, then remove it carefully once the cutting is sealed.
    • 0.1 mL pipettes are hard to read. Consider the whole-plant method if potometer assembly stalls.
    • Peels need no stain; close the condenser diaphragm to increase contrast.
    • Use the leaf-tracing method rather than leaf mass. Leaf thickness varies, so mass-based area estimates differ a lot between groups.
    How the simulation maps to the bench

    Model, procedure, and limits

    Leaf area. A random leaf outline (bean, Coleus, Impatiens, or tomato leaflet; 5–30 cm²) is drawn on 0.5 cm grid paper. Students type their counts of full and partial squares; the page computes (full + ½ partial)/4 and compares it to the exact polygon area ("planimeter value"). A checkbox shades full and partial squares so students can check their count.

    Stomatal peel. Ten species with the manual's Table 1 densities, upper or lower surface, 100X (field 1.8 mm) or 400X (field 0.45 mm, 0.159 mm²). Each field draws a Poisson count around the species density (with ~15% patch variation), spaced so stomata never touch, set in a jigsaw Voronoi epidermis (elongated cells and rows for corn and oats). Students click stomata to tally them; recording a field shows the true count beside theirs. The density check needs at least three 400X fields.

    Transpiration model. E = g·VPD/P with 1/g = 1/gs + 1/gb. VPD uses the Tetens equation at leaf temperature; gs = gmax(species) × a light response × a VPD-closure term × a salt term × the fraction of stomata left unsealed by petroleum jelly (× a soil-water term in the whole-plant method); gb = 0.19√(u/0.05). Each plant gets a lognormal gmax (CV ≈ 18%) and leaf area ±15%. Instrument noise: pipette ±0.005 mL, balance ±0.1 g, pressure ±0.01 kPa. Methods: potometer (readings every 3 min for 30 min), pressure sensor (every minute for 20 min; 8 mL of air in the line), and whole plant (daily mass for 4 days with 14 h light / 10 h dark).

    Students fill in rate ÷ leaf area themselves (scaled ×104, ×103, or ×102, as Step 5 in the manual suggests), and a check button grades the entries. The class table computes means, ±2 SE, adjusted rate (treatment − control), and a Welch t-test vs. control, following the manual's Step 6.

    Limits to discuss
    • The model ignores stomatal response time, xylem cavitation, and leaf wilting dynamics. Real fans also cool leaves, and real lamps heat air unevenly.
    • Cuttings in the sim never get air-locked. On the bench, air bubbles and stem gaps are the main cause of failed runs.
    • In the whole-plant method, the soil water reservoir (≈ 220 g available) can run low by days 3–4 under strong treatments, which compresses differences between fan, lamp, and control. This is a good discussion point about controlling soil water.
    • Units differ by method; compare treatments only within one method and one species.
    Common misconceptions

    What students often get wrong

    • "Roots push water up." Root pressure is small; the main driver is the tension created by evaporation from leaves (TACT).
    • "Plants use most of the water they absorb." Most is transpired; only a small fraction is used in growth and photosynthesis.
    • "Stomata are there to lose water." They exist to admit CO2; water loss is the cost.
    • "Double the traced area because leaves have two sides." Report one-side (projected) area. Stomata are usually concentrated on one surface (often the lower), and transpiration rates are conventionally per one-side area.
    • "Heat always increases transpiration." Heat raises VPD, but very high VPD or water stress makes guard cells close stomata (abscisic acid), limiting the rise.
    • "Water moves toward more water." Water moves toward lower water potential, which usually means more solute or more tension.
    Suggested answers

    Pre-lab, procedure questions, and analysis

    Getting Started

    1. Cell with lower ψ than surroundings.
    The cell gains water. The diagram should show ψoutside > ψcell, an arrow inward, and turgor pressure building until ψ is equal.
    2. Road salt and grass.
    Salt makes ψS of soil water very negative, so ψsoil < ψroot; roots cannot absorb water and may lose it (plasmolysis); grass dies of water stress.
    3. Xylem vs. phloem.
    Xylem: dead, hollow, lignified tracheids and vessel elements forming continuous tubes that withstand tension. Phloem: living sieve-tube elements with sieve plates, supported by companion cells, for active sugar loading and pressure-flow transport.
    4. Transplanting a tree.
    In winter (or when dormant and leafless). Transpiration is minimal, so the damaged roots do not need to supply much water.
    5–7. Calculations.
    0.159 mm²; 28 ÷ 0.159 = 176 stomata/mm²; (92 + 15)/4 = 26.75 cm² = 2675 mm².
    8. CO2 and stomatal density.
    High CO2 selects for fewer stomata (enough CO2 enters with less water loss); low CO2 selects for more.

    Procedure Step 1

    a–b.
    Trace and count squares (÷ 4 for cm², × 100 for mm²). Estimate a whole plant's leaf area as mean area of a sample of leaves × number of leaves.
    c–e.
    More stomata per mm² and more leaf area mean more total water loss; fewer or smaller leaves reduce total transpiration (shown by the "leaves left" slider; the rate per cm² stays similar).
    f.
    Generally no: use one-side area, because stomata are mostly on one side (Table 1) and rates are compared per projected area. Accept a well-argued alternative if applied consistently.
    h.
    Yes. Water lily (floating leaves) has 460/mm² on the upper surface only, where it touches air; submerged Anacharis has none; most land plants put most stomata on the shaded, cooler lower surface, which reduces water loss. Grasses (oats, corn) with upright leaves have stomata on both sides.

    Expect the lamp and fan to raise transpiration by roughly 1.5–1.8×, the misted bag to cut it to about 15%, and darkness to about 25–30% (cuticular loss plus some stomatal leakage). Oleander and fern transpire much less than Impatiens or bean. Sealing the lower surface of Impatiens or bean cuts transpiration by about 60–75%; sealing the upper surface barely matters.

    Analyzing results

    1–3.
    Answers depend on data. The heat lamp or fan usually gives the largest increase. Lamp: raises leaf temperature (higher saturation vapor pressure, larger VPD) and opens stomata in bright light. Fan: thins the boundary layer, raising total conductance. Humidity lowers VPD toward zero.
    4. Why leaf area?
    Plants and cuttings differ in size; dividing by area makes rates comparable between plants and groups.
    5. Adaptations.
    Waxy cuticle, stomata on the lower surface or sunken in pits (oleander), trichomes that thicken the boundary layer, fewer or smaller leaves, leaf shedding, CAM or C4 photosynthesis, guard-cell closing triggered by abscisic acid and K+ efflux.
    6. Stomata number.
    Predict a positive relationship. Test by comparing species of known density, or by sealing one surface with petroleum jelly (sim: jelly option).
    7. TACT in a 100-ft tree.
    Evaporation in leaves creates tension; cohesion (hydrogen bonds) transmits it down continuous xylem columns; adhesion to narrow xylem walls resists the column breaking and gravity. A large temperature rise increases VPD and transpiration until water stress triggers guard cells to close stomata, which then cuts transpiration (and CO2 uptake).
    Student-designed investigations

    Ideas that work well

    • Wind dose-response: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 m/s, 4+ replicates; graph means ± 2 SE and fit a curve. The response saturates because stomata become limiting.
    • Humidity: RH 30–90%; rate falls roughly linearly with VPD.
    • Light intensity: 0–1200 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹; a saturating stomatal light response.
    • Salt: 0–0.3 M NaCl; rate falls as root water uptake becomes harder (connects to the road-salt question).
    • Habitat comparison: Impatiens vs. oleander or fern under identical conditions (the manual's "Where can you go" #2).
    • Structure: petroleum jelly on upper vs. lower surfaces, or removing 25–75% of leaves, linking back to the stomatal density data.

    The t-test panel compares any two groups (Welch's t, two-tailed). Remind students to change one variable at a time; all trials share one table, and grouping only separates the variable they choose.

    Extensions

    Where students can go next

    • Investigate guard-cell control: the roles of K+ uptake, abscisic acid, and blue light.
    • Compare stomatal density of sun and shade leaves on the same campus tree, or of herbarium leaves collected decades apart (rising CO2).
    • Thin stem sections to identify xylem and phloem and relate structure to function.
    • Read about the synthetic "tree" that moves water under negative pressure (Wheeler and Stroock, Nature 2008).
    • Summative ideas from the manual: a class graph of all variables, a mini-lesson on transpiration for younger students, a list of misconceptions students held before the lab, and student-written AP-style questions.
