Fruit Fly Behavior

What environmental factors trigger a fruit fly response?

Unit 8: EcologyENE-3.DIST-5.ASYI-1.HScience Practices 3.C, 5.A
    At a glance

    Planning summary

    TimingTwo to four 50-minute periods. Day 1: sexing, life cycle, vial geotaxis, chamber construction. Day 2: choice-chamber trials with household substances. Days 3–4: student-designed investigations and presentations. Chi-square and data tables may need time outside class.

    Prior knowledgeComplete metamorphosis, apoptosis in metamorphosis, genetic basis of behavior, taxis, environmental triggers of behavior, basic sensory organ and neuron structure.
    SafetyDispense chemicals in labeled dropper bottles only (no stock bottles of vinegar, ammonia, bleach); lab acids/bases no more than 0.1 M; goggles for liquids; ethanol away from flames; no eating test foods; flies to a morgue (oil or 70% alcohol), never released.
    Preparation

    Materials and setup

    • Fruit fly cultures: about 30–40 flies per group per day. Order at least two weeks ahead if students will compare fly ages (isolate adults into a new vial to age them; new flies emerge about two weeks later).
    • Choice chambers: two matching 12–16 oz soft plastic water bottles per group, bottoms cut off, dried, taped end to end with clear packing tape, ends labeled A and B. At least six extra caps per group.
    • Cotton balls (about 20 per group) and tape; droppers; timers; dissecting microscopes; small (about 20 mL) amounts of test substances: condiments (capers, mustard, ketchup, mayonnaise, salad dressing, jelly, peanut butter), yeast, fruits (ripe and green banana, apple, melon), and lab chemicals (ethanol, dilute ammonia, distilled water).
    • Tips: 5–10 drops per cotton ball (more drips and sticks flies to the bottle); change or clean and dry caps between substances; chill vials 15–30 min before tossing flies to slow them, without adding moisture. A funnel trap with vinegar or banana recaptures escapees.
    • For larva experiments: 2% non-nutrient agar (2 g in 100 mL water) or gelatin on a slide.

    Important: the manual asks you not to tell students in advance that vinegar and alcohol (fermentation products) are the common attractants. The student page never names them as the answer; it lists substances and lets students find the pattern.

    How the simulation maps to the real procedure

    What is real, what is modeled

    • Sexing practice: drawn dorsal views (not photos) of females (larger, pointed abdomen, separate stripes, light tip) and males (smaller, rounded, fused dark tip, sex combs). Use real flies under dissecting microscopes too; the manual recommends clear photographs over diagrams.
    • Vial geotaxis: flies start at the bottom after tapping and climb at individual speeds; 10% do not respond; 4-week-old flies climb about half as fast (age-related decline, as in RING assays).
    • Choice chamber: each fly does a persistent random walk with walking, resting, and short flights. Stimuli create a preference landscape along the chamber: odors decay exponentially from each cotton ball; light and gravity are linear gradients. Steps up the landscape are always taken; steps down are taken with probability eΔΦ, otherwise the fly turns back (an oriented, taxis-like response). Each stimulus strength is calibrated so that it alone vs. water gives a target split after 5 minutes, and flies reach that split in about 3–5 simulated minutes.
    • Calibrated targets (% of flies at the substance end, vs. water): apple cider vinegar ≈ 84, rotting banana ≈ 80, yeast + sugar ≈ 79, ripe banana ≈ 73, capers ≈ 71, mustard ≈ 70, 10% ethanol ≈ 68, ketchup ≈ 65, mayonnaise ≈ 62, jelly ≈ 58, green banana ≈ 56, peanut butter ≈ 53, water 50, Alka-Seltzer (CO2) ≈ 36, dilute ammonia ≈ 22. Lamp at one end ≈ 68 (red film ≈ 54; white-eyed flies respond at one quarter strength). Upright chamber: ≈ 80 at the top (≈ 66 for old flies). Parafilm cuts odor effects to 10%. Adh-negative flies avoid ethanol (≈ 38). Vestigial flies cannot fly and settle more slowly.
    • Position control: a weak hidden room-light bias (≈ 53:47 toward one screen side, chosen at random on page load) exists when the chamber lies flat. Students can detect it only by pooling water/water trials and swapping ends, which mirrors the manual's advice to rotate the chamber.
    • Counting: by default the trial pauses at each interval and hides the live count; students type their own counts, and actual counts are revealed when the trial ends. The final-time counts go into a chi-square test (50:50 null, df = 1).
    • Limits: no sex differences, no circadian or temperature effects, no fly escapes or sticking in liquid, and no learning or habituation. Combined stimuli simply add.
    Common misconceptions

    Watch for these

    • "Fruit flies are attracted to fruit." They are attracted mainly to fermentation and decay products (acetic acid, ethanol) from yeasts on rotting fruit. Ripe banana attracts less than rotting banana; green banana barely at all; vinegar-containing condiments attract strongly.
    • Taxis vs. kinesis. Students call any accumulation a taxis. Ask how the flies got there: oriented movement (taxis) or a change in speed or turning rate (kinesis)?
    • "Geotaxis is positive because they respond to gravity." Positive/negative refers to direction: flies climb against gravity, a negative geotaxis.
    • Chi-square proves the hypothesis. It only tells whether the data are unlikely under the null hypothesis. Failing to reject is not proof of no preference, especially with small n.
    • Chi-square on percentages. Use counts, not percentages.
    • One trial is enough. Replicate and swap ends to separate substance effects from position effects.

    Vial geotaxis

    Most flies (typically 10–14 of 15) are in the top half by 15 s and stay there at 30 s. The response is an oriented movement against gravity: a negative geotaxis. A chemical cause is unlikely because inverting or tapping changes only orientation; testing a sealed, empty vial on its side would show no up/down pattern.

    Analysis and evaluation

    • Attractants share fermentation/decay products: acetic acid (vinegar in mustard, ketchup, capers, mayonnaise) and ethanol, plus yeast activity. These signal food (yeast) and egg-laying sites, raising fitness (IST-5.A).
    • Repellents (ammonia, CO2) signal decay hazards or crowding stress; flies avoid them.
    • In kitchens, flies gather at fruit bowls, compost, wine glasses, and condiments because of these odors. Data do not explain all movement (light, gravity, mating, temperature, and individual variation also matter). "Vinegar flies" is arguably a better name.
    • Controls: same chamber and flies, same drop volume, cap positions swapped, lighting and orientation held constant, same time before counting.
    • Phototaxis vs. chemotaxis: a lamp at the water end partly offsets vinegar; geotaxis in an upright chamber can override weak attractants.
    Ideas for student-designed investigations

    What students can test

    • Isolate ingredients from a label (vinegar, sugar, salt) and test each.
    • Smell vs. sight: Parafilm over the cotton ball (in the sim, odor drops to 10%).
    • Light color: colored films on one end; red light should give a weak response.
    • Ripeness: ripe vs. green vs. rotting banana.
    • CO2: Alka-Seltzer in moist cotton.
    • Age: young vs. 4-week flies in the geotaxis vial or an upright chamber.
    • Mutants: vestigial (slower to reach ends, easier to count), white-eyed (weaker phototaxis), Adh-negative (response to alcohol). Sex differences using F1 white-eyed males and red-eyed females (bench only).
    • Competing stimuli: does phototaxis or geotaxis override chemotaxis?
    • Larvae on agar slides: chemotaxis and negative phototaxis (bench only).

    Require several trials per condition, swapped ends, and chi-square on pooled counts.

    Extensions

    Where students can go from here

    • Repeat with ladybugs, houseflies, or mealworms and predict which will behave like fruit flies.
    • Build a chamber with more than two choices and use chi-square with more degrees of freedom.
    • Read about Seymour Benzer's behavior genetics (Weiner, Time, Love, Memory) or larval odor navigation (Raman et al., 2008).
    • Use FlyBase to look up the genes behind the mutants students tested.
    • Released AP free-response questions on behavior (e.g., the 1997 sea slug question) make good summative assessments.
